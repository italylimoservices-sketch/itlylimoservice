Every Italian city runs its buses, trams, and metro lines a little differently, but the underlying logic is the same everywhere — and it trips up a lot of first-time visitors. You buy a ticket before you board, not from a driver. You validate it the moment you get on, not when you feel like it. And a single ticket usually isn't a single ride — it's a block of time (75, 90, or 100 minutes depending on the city) during which you can hop between buses and trams freely, with the metro often being the one exception that only lets you enter once per ticket.

This guide covers how to actually use public transit inside Italy's main cities: Rome, Milan, Naples, Turin, Venice, Florence, and Bologna. It doesn't cover how to travel *between* these cities — for that, see our [Italy train travel guide](/blog/italy-train-travel-guide). This is strictly about what happens once you've already arrived and need to get from your hotel to the Colosseum, or from the station to your Airbnb.

## How Italian city transit tickets actually work

Almost every Italian city uses some version of the same integrated ticket system, usually called a BIT (Biglietto Integrato a Tempo) or the local equivalent. A few things to know before you land:

- **Buy before you board.** Tickets are sold at tobacco shops (tabacchi, marked with a black-and-white "T" sign), newsstands, vending machines at metro stations and major stops, and increasingly through city transit apps. Bus and tram drivers generally do not sell tickets, or if they do, it costs more and requires exact change.
- **Validate immediately.** Every ticket must be stamped in a small validation machine — a yellow or green box near the doors on buses and trams, or at the turnstile/platform for metro. Validation timestamps the ticket and starts its countdown. An unvalidated ticket, even one you legitimately paid for, is treated as invalid if an inspector checks it.
- **Time-based, not ride-based.** A ticket bought for 90 or 100 minutes lets you transfer between buses and trams as many times as you like within that window. The usual catch is the metro: most cities only allow one metro entry per ticket, even if time remains afterward.
- **Contactless is spreading fast.** Rome, Turin, and Milan increasingly let you tap a contactless bank card or phone directly at metro gates and validators, with the fare charged automatically — sometimes at a slight discount over paper tickets, and often with a daily fare cap.
- **Fines are real and not negotiable on the spot.** Inspectors in plain clothes do random checks, especially on buses and trams where there's no gate to stop you from boarding without a ticket. Fines for an unvalidated or missing ticket typically start around €50 and can run into the hundreds depending on the city and whether the inspector judges it as intentional fare evasion.

None of these systems are built with luggage in mind — buses and trams get crowded, there's rarely dedicated space for suitcases, and metro stations are not always step-free. That's a big part of why most travelers arriving with bags use a [private airport transfer](/airport-transfers) or [taxi](/blog/italy-taxi-private-transfer-guide) to get into the city center, then switch to public transit for day-to-day sightseeing once they're unpacked.

## Rome

Rome's public transit is run by ATAC and covers metro, buses, and trams under one ticketing system.

- **Metro:** Three lines — A (orange), B with its B1 branch (blue), and C (green, still expanding toward the center). The network is smaller than you'd expect for a capital city, partly because archaeological finds keep interrupting tunneling projects.
- **Buses and trams:** This is where most of Rome's transit actually happens. Buses fill the gaps the metro doesn't reach, including most of the historic center, and a handful of tram lines serve neighborhoods like Trastevere and San Lorenzo.
- **Tickets:** A single BIT ticket costs €1.50 and is valid for 100 minutes, covering unlimited bus and tram transfers plus one metro ride. Tap-to-pay with a contactless card or phone works directly at metro turnstiles and on validators, with a daily fare cap around €8.50. Multi-day options include 24-hour, 48-hour, 72-hour, and weekly passes for visitors planning to move around a lot.
- **Getting around the sights:** Central Rome is compact and very walkable, and for many travelers the metro plus a lot of walking covers the major sights near [Rome](/destinations/rome). Buses fill in the rest.

## Milan

Milan has the most extensive rapid transit network in Italy, run by ATM.

- **Metro:** Five lines (M1 red, M2 green, M3 yellow, M4 blue, M5 purple) covering roughly 95 km and over 100 stations. M4 and M5 are fully automated and driverless; the older M1–M3 use conventional trains with drivers.
- **Trams and buses:** Milan also runs a large tram network, including some vintage orange streetcars still in daily service, alongside an extensive bus network filling in areas the metro doesn't reach.
- **Tickets:** A single ticket costs €2.20 and is valid for 90 minutes, with unlimited surface transfers but one metro ride. A 24-hour pass runs about €7.60, and a booklet of 10 tickets is available at a discount for anyone staying a few days.
- **Practical note:** [Milan's](/destinations/milan) metro connects efficiently to both of the city's airports via dedicated lines and shuttle buses, but for arrivals with luggage straight off a flight, most visitors still prefer a car or transfer rather than hauling bags through metro turnstiles at rush hour.

## Naples

Naples has a smaller metro than Rome or Milan, but it's paired with something no other Italian city has at this scale: a working network of historic funiculars.

- **Metro:** Two main lines (1 and 6) run by ANM, with some stations doubling as public art installations — several are genuinely worth a look even if you're not going anywhere.
- **Funiculars:** Four separate funicular lines — Centrale, Chiaia, Montesanto, and Mergellina — climb from the lower city up to the Vomero hill district. The oldest, Chiaia, opened in 1889 and is still one of the most efficient ways to reach Vomero's viewpoints without a long uphill walk.
- **Tickets:** A single UnicoCampania ticket covers buses, the funiculars, and Metro Lines 1 and 6, with metro rides priced slightly higher (around €1.50) than bus and funicular-only tickets (around €1.30). A 90-minute integrated ticket covering transfers across modes costs about €1.80, and day passes are available for around €4.50–€5.40.
- **Reality check:** Naples' surface traffic is notoriously chaotic, and buses can be unpredictable with timing. Many visitors to [Naples](/destinations/naples) rely on the metro and funiculars for reliable point-to-point trips and walk or take a taxi for anything off that grid.

## Turin

Turin pairs Italy's first driverless metro with one of the country's largest tram networks, both run by GTT.

- **Metro:** A single line (Line 1) using automated VAL technology, running about 15 km through 23 stations from the western suburbs through the center and down toward the southern districts. It was Italy's first fully automated metro when it opened in 2006.
- **Trams:** This is Turin's backbone. The tram network is extensive and dense, covering most of the city grid that the single metro line can't, including some routes running on classic Italian streetcars.
- **Tickets:** A single ticket ("City 100") is valid for 100 minutes across metro, tram, and bus. It costs about €1.90 bought digitally through the city's transit app or via contactless tap, versus around €2.00 for a paper ticket from a tobacco shop or machine. Daily and multi-day tourist passes are also available.
- **Layout tip:** Much of central [Turin's](/destinations/turin) historic core — its arcaded streets and grand piazzas — is best covered on foot, with the tram network filling in longer hops between neighborhoods.

## Venice

Venice is the one city on this list where the usual rules don't apply, because there are no roads to run buses on in the historic center.

- **The vaporetto is the public transit system.** ACTV's water buses are the equivalent of Rome's metro or Milan's trams — the main way to cross the city or reach the outer islands without walking the entire route. Line 1 runs the length of the Grand Canal and is effectively Venice's signature transit line, doubling as a slow, scenic way to see the city.
- **Walking is the other half of the system.** Venice's alleys connect almost everywhere within the historic center faster than the vaporetto can, since the boats have to follow the canals. Locals walk far more than they ride.
- **Tickets are expensive by Italian standards.** A single vaporetto ticket costs around €9.50 and is valid for about 75 minutes — noticeably pricier than a bus ticket in Rome or Milan, reflecting Venice's status as a heavily touristed, logistically unique city. Multi-day Tourist Travel Cards are the better value if you're riding more than once or twice a day: roughly €25 for 24 hours, €35 for 48 hours, €45 for 72 hours, and €65 for seven days, covering vaporetto, mainland buses in Mestre, the tram, and the People Mover.
- **Younger travelers get a discount.** The Rolling Venice Card (around €6, for visitors aged 6–29) unlocks a discounted 72-hour pass.
- **Luggage and boats don't mix well.** Vaporetti get packed, decks are exposed to weather, and dragging a suitcase on and off a rocking boat at a crowded stop is genuinely awkward. Most people arriving in [Venice](/destinations/venice) with luggage take a water taxi or private transfer directly to their hotel, then use vaporetto tickets purely for sightseeing days once they've dropped their bags.

## Florence and Bologna: bus-first cities

Neither Florence nor Bologna has a metro system, and both rely primarily on buses — though Florence is the exception with a growing tram network.

**Florence (ATAF):**
- Buses cover the vast majority of routes, but [Florence](/destinations/florence) also runs two tram lines — T1 (Leonardo), linking the southern suburbs through Santa Maria Novella station up to Careggi, and T2 (Vespucci), connecting the station to the airport. A third line, T3, is under construction and expected to open in late 2026 or 2027.
- A single ticket costs €2.00 and is valid for 90 minutes across both buses and trams. A pack of 10 tickets is available at a discount for longer stays.
- The historic center is small enough that most sightseeing is done on foot; buses and the tram mainly matter for reaching the train station, the airport, or outlying neighborhoods.

**Bologna (TPER):**
- Bologna runs a bus-only network (no tram or metro), with a compact historic center under Italy's longest stretch of covered porticoes, making walking especially pleasant even in bad weather.
- A single ticket bought in advance costs around €1.50 and is valid for 75 minutes; buying directly from the driver on board costs more, around €2.
- As in other cities, tickets must be validated on boarding, with fines ranging roughly €60–€200 for skipping this step. Given how walkable central [Bologna](/destinations/bologna) is, many visitors barely need the bus system beyond reaching the train station or the airport shuttle.

## Tips for getting around without the headaches

- **Buy a few tickets at once.** If you're staying somewhere without a metro station or tabacchi nearby, grab several tickets in advance so you're not searching for an open shop right when you need to board.
- **Validate every single time**, even if you already validated a ticket earlier that day for a different mode — some cities require re-validation at each transfer despite the ticket still being technically valid.
- **Avoid rush hour if you can.** Weekday mornings (roughly 7:30–9:30) and early evenings (5:00–7:30) are when metro cars and buses in Rome and Milan get genuinely packed, which is unpleasant with any amount of luggage.
- **Check whether your day involves a lot of luggage-carrying.** Airport arrivals, hotel-to-hotel moves between cities, or heading to a train station with bags are the moments where a [private chauffeur](/chauffeur-service) is worth the extra cost over wrestling a suitcase through a crowded bus.
- **Download the local app before you land.** ATAC (Rome), ATM (Milan), UnicoCampania (Naples), GTT's To Move (Turin), and AVM/ACTV (Venice) all offer digital tickets and route planning, which is faster than hunting for an open tabacchi late at night.
- **Keep your ticket until you exit.** Even on systems without exit gates, inspectors can check tickets mid-journey, and "I already got off" isn't an accepted excuse for an unvalidated one.

Public transit works well for daily sightseeing in every one of these cities once you understand the ticket-and-validate rhythm — it's really the getting-there-with-luggage part, and the between-city travel, where it makes more sense to book a car in advance. For that side of the trip, our guide to [intercity train travel](/blog/italy-train-travel-guide) covers what to expect.
