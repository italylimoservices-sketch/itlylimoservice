## What Pompeii Actually Involves

Pompeii was a working Roman city before the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD buried it in volcanic ash, and what survived is an entire city rather than a single monument — streets, houses, public buildings, and plazas spread across a genuinely large area. That scale is the single most important thing to plan around. This isn't a site you walk through in forty minutes; it's one where visitors regularly underestimate both the distance covered and the surface underfoot.

The paving throughout much of the site is original ancient stone, which has had two thousand years to become uneven, worn smooth in places, and genuinely awkward to walk on for any length of time. There's also very little shade across large stretches, which becomes a real factor rather than a minor inconvenience once the sun is high, particularly from late spring through summer.

## Why Footwear Matters More Than Most Visitors Expect

It's worth being specific about this because it's the single most common thing travelers get wrong at Pompeii: sandals, dress shoes, or anything without real support are a poor match for the site. The ground is uneven ancient stone for the vast majority of the visit, not paved museum flooring, and a few hours of it adds up quickly. Sturdy, broken-in walking shoes make a genuine difference to how the visit feels by the end, especially if you're planning to see a meaningful portion of the site rather than a quick highlights loop.

Alongside footwear, it's worth carrying water rather than assuming it will be easy to find at every turn, and having some sun protection given how exposed most of the site is. None of this is unusual advice, but it's the kind of thing that's easy to forget while focused on transport and timing, and the kind of thing you notice immediately once you're an hour into walking under an open sky.

## Timing a Half-Day Visit

A half-day is workable at Pompeii, but it rewards a reasonably early start. Arriving earlier in the day means cooler temperatures, fewer crowds along the main routes through the site, and more comfortable walking conditions generally — all of which matter more here than they would at a smaller or more sheltered attraction. If your schedule allows some flexibility, building the departure from Sorrento around an earlier arrival at the ruins tends to make for a noticeably better visit than a later start squeezed in after breakfast.

It's also worth being realistic about how much of the site you actually want to see. Pompeii rewards a focused visit to its more significant areas over an attempt to cover the entire excavated city, particularly if half a day is the time available. A private driver can wait for you regardless of exactly how long that focused visit ends up taking, which is one of the clearer advantages over public transport options with a fixed timetable to work around.
