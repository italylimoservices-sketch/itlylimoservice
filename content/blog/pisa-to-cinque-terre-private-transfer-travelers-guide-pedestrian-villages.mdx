There's one thing almost every first-time visitor to Cinque Terre doesn't fully grasp until they're standing at the edge of it, bags in hand: none of the five villages are built for cars. Monterosso, Vernazza, Corniglia, Manarola and Riomaggiore are pedestrian places, threaded through with stairs, narrow lanes and cliffside paths, and no private vehicle — however good the driver — can take you to your hotel's front door the way it could almost anywhere else in Italy. If you understand that one fact before you book, the rest of the trip from Pisa falls into place easily. If you don't, it can catch you off guard on arrival.

## The Core Thing to Understand

Cinque Terre's villages grew up on steep terrain along a stretch of Ligurian coastline, long before cars existed, and they were never retrofitted for vehicle access the way some historic centers elsewhere have been. There are no roads running into the heart of any of the five villages, and parking directly at village edges is limited, often restricted to residents, and rarely a realistic option for a visitor arriving with luggage. This isn't a minor inconvenience — it's the defining fact of how transport into this part of Liguria works, private car or not.

What that means practically: a private transfer from Pisa can get you to the region, but it will drop you at a gateway town rather than inside a village. From there, the final stretch is covered by the regional train that runs along the coast connecting La Spezia, the five villages themselves, and Levanto at the northern end of the line. Walking is the alternative in some cases — a few villages are close enough to reach on the coastal paths — but for most travelers with bags, the train is the realistic option.

## Where a Private Transfer Actually Drops You

Most private transfers from Pisa Airport end at La Spezia or Levanto, the two main gateway towns bracketing the Cinque Terre train line. Both have proper road access and parking, unlike the villages themselves, which is exactly why they function as the handoff point.

- **La Spezia** sits at the southern end of the line, is the larger of the two towns, and also works as a cruise port, so it tends to have more hotels, restaurants and general infrastructure.
- **Levanto** sits at the northern end, is smaller, and puts you slightly closer to Monterosso, the northernmost of the five villages.

Which one makes sense depends on your plans — whether you're staying in the gateway town and day-tripping into the villages, or pushing on the same day to a specific village where you've booked accommodation. Either way, your driver can drop you close to the relevant train station, but confirm this detail when booking, since "close to the station" can still mean a short walk with bags depending on exactly where traffic is allowed to stop.

## What Happens After the Drop-Off

Once you're at the gateway town, the regional train is the standard way to continue. It's a short, frequent-enough service connecting La Spezia, the five villages, and Levanto, and it's genuinely one of the easier regional trains to use in Italy — platforms are clearly marked, journeys between adjacent villages take only a few minutes, and the route itself runs along a dramatic stretch of coastline that's worth the ride on its own.

A few practical things worth knowing before you arrive:

- **Buy your ticket before boarding.** Validate it at the machine on the platform if that's how the local system works that day — ticket enforcement varies, but it's not worth risking a fine over.
- **Travel light for the final stretch if you can.** Village streets are often stairs, and not every accommodation is close to the train platform. A smaller bag or a soft-sided suitcase is considerably easier to manage than a large hard-shell case on cobblestones and steps.
- **Expect crowding in peak season.** Midday trains in summer can get full, and standing for a few stops isn't unusual. If you have a choice, earlier or later trains tend to be more comfortable.
- **Confirm which station is closest to your accommodation.** Village centers are small, but "small" doesn't mean flat — Corniglia, for instance, sits above its train station and requires a walk up a number of steps or a local shuttle to reach the village itself.

## Why This Isn't a Flaw in the Plan

It's easy to read all this and think the pedestrian restriction is an obstacle to work around, but it's really the reason Cinque Terre looks the way it does. The absence of roads through the villages is part of what's preserved their character — no traffic noise in the lanes, no parking lots breaking up the cliffside views, and a pace of walking that matches the terrain rather than fighting it. The private transfer and the train aren't competing solutions to the same problem; they're each doing the part of the journey they're actually suited for; the car covers the longer distance from the airport efficiently, and the train covers the short, scenic final stretch that no vehicle could manage anyway.

## Planning Around This if You're Staying Multiple Nights

If your stay in the region spans several days, it's worth deciding early whether you want to base yourself in a gateway town and day-trip in by train, or stay inside one of the villages and deal with the walking and stairs as part of daily life there. Both are common approaches. Staying in a gateway town like La Spezia tends to suit travelers who want more amenities and an easier time managing luggage across a multi-night stay, while staying inside a village like Vernazza or Manarola suits travelers who want to wake up in the middle of the scenery and don't mind the walking that comes with it.

## Choosing a Vehicle With This Trip in Mind

Because luggage has to transition from car to train partway through this journey, it's worth thinking about vehicle choice with that handoff in mind rather than just passenger count. A couple traveling light is comfortable in an [executive sedan](/fleet/executive-sedan) or [luxury sedan](/fleet/luxury-sedan). Families or groups with more bags often prefer a [luxury SUV](/fleet/luxury-suv), which gives more room to sort luggage before the walk to the platform. For a larger group moving together, a [luxury van](/fleet/luxury-van) keeps everyone in one vehicle for the Pisa-to-gateway leg, even though the group will split the remaining distance by train or on foot.

## The One Thing to Remember

If there's a single takeaway for this route, it's this: don't expect a private transfer to replace the train for the final stretch into the villages, and don't book accommodation deep inside a village without accounting for the walk from the nearest station. Once that's clear, the rest of the trip — a comfortable drive from Pisa Airport, a short scenic train ride, and a walk into one of Italy's most distinctive coastal landscapes — is straightforward to plan.
