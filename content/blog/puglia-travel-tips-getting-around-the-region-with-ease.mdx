First-time visitors to Puglia tend to arrive with a map full of names — Alberobello, Ostuni, Lecce, Polignano a Mare, the Valle d'Itria — and a less clear idea of how those places actually connect to each other day to day. That gap is normal. Puglia doesn't have one obvious hub city the way some Italian regions do, and figuring out how to move between its towns is worth sorting out before the trip starts rather than during it.

## Start With the Two Gateways

Most visitors arrive through one of Puglia's two main airports: Bari or Brindisi. Which one makes sense depends on where the trip is actually centered — a plan weighted toward the Valle d'Itria and the Adriatic coast north of Brindisi might favor one gateway, while a trip focused more on the Salento peninsula in the south might favor the other. Our [Bari destination guide](/destinations/bari) is a useful starting point if that city is part of the plan, whether as the arrival point, a stop along the way, or both.

There's no single verified travel time between any two Puglia towns worth stating here as fact — road conditions, the exact route, and season all affect it, and a specific number would be a guess rather than something reliably true. What's safe to say is that Puglia's layout means a trip touching several towns involves real time on the road, and that's worth building into the itinerary from day one rather than discovering once you're there.

## Self-Driving: When It Makes Sense

Renting a car is a reasonable option for some Puglia trips, particularly ones built around flexibility over a longer stay, with a base in the countryside and day trips radiating out from it at your own pace. Self-driving suits travelers who are comfortable navigating unfamiliar roads, don't mind figuring out parking on the fly, and are planning a trip where no single day is especially packed with multiple stops.

It's worth being realistic about where self-driving gets harder, though. Puglia's historic town centers — Alberobello's trulli district, Ostuni's old town, Lecce's baroque core — often have narrow streets with limited vehicle access, which means a rental car frequently can't get anywhere near where you're actually trying to go inside these places. Finding parking near (not in) these centers, then walking the rest of the way, becomes part of the routine. That's manageable for some travelers and genuinely tiring for others, particularly with young kids, more luggage, or multiple stops packed into one day.

## When a Private Chauffeur Is Worth It

A private chauffeur doesn't eliminate Puglia's narrow streets or its spread-out towns — nothing does. What changes is who's dealing with the navigation, the parking search, and the walk-in logistics, and how much the day can bend around your actual pace rather than a fixed plan.

This tends to matter most in a few specific situations:

- **Multi-stop touring days.** A day that includes two or three towns runs more smoothly when someone who knows the roads and the realistic drop-off points is handling the route, rather than you figuring it out live between stops.
- **Airport transfers.** Arriving at Bari or Brindisi after a long flight and immediately managing a rental counter, then unfamiliar roads, is a rougher start than being met at arrivals with a vehicle already sized for your group and luggage.
- **Groups and families.** Larger groups, multi-generational trips, and families with young children all benefit from not having the driving itself be one more thing to coordinate. Our guides on [family travel in Puglia](/blog/family-travel-in-puglia-why-a-private-chauffeur-can-help) and [private transportation for families and groups](/blog/puglia-private-transportation-for-families-and-groups) go into those cases specifically.
- **Evenings that involve wine.** Puglia's countryside is dotted with wineries and masserie worth an evening visit, and not having to drive back afterward is a straightforward practical advantage.

## A Realistic Way to Think About It

Neither option is objectively correct — it depends on the shape of the trip. A traveler spending ten days based in one countryside villa, making occasional unhurried day trips, may be perfectly well served by a rental car. A traveler trying to cover several towns in Puglia within three or four days, especially with family along or significant luggage, is usually better served by a private transfer that removes the navigation and parking search from an already tight schedule.

A useful middle-ground question to ask yourself: how many separate stops does a typical day in this itinerary actually involve? One stop a day leans toward self-driving being manageable. Two or three leans toward a private driver making a real difference, particularly once Puglia's narrow old-town streets are factored into each stop.

## Orientation Basics for First-Time Visitors

A few general points help regardless of how you end up getting around:

- Puglia's historic centers are generally best explored on foot once you've arrived — plan for a vehicle to get you close, not necessarily all the way in
- The region doesn't have one single hub town; Bari, the Valle d'Itria towns, and the Salento peninsula each have a somewhat different character and distance relationship to the others
- Countryside roads connecting towns pass through genuinely rural stretches — olive groves, dry stone walls, small farming villages — which is part of the appeal but also means fewer services along the way than a traveler might expect from a more built-up region
- Building extra time into any day with more than one stop is a safer assumption than planning around a best-case timeline

If you do go the private transfer route, the vehicle question is worth a moment's thought rather than defaulting to whatever seems standard. None of these choices need to be locked in before you request a quote. Giving an honest passenger and luggage count is usually enough for the right option to be suggested back to you.

## Mixing Approaches Across a Longer Trip

It's also worth knowing that self-driving and private transfers aren't mutually exclusive across a single trip. Some visitors rent a car for a relaxed, villa-based middle stretch of their visit, then arrange a private transfer specifically for the airport legs and for any day involving multiple towns or a late-evening winery visit where driving back isn't appealing. Thinking about transportation per-day rather than as one fixed choice for the whole trip often ends up being the most practical approach, especially on a visit longer than four or five days.
