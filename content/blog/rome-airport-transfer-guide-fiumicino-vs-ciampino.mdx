Rome has two airports, and travelers often assume they're interchangeable. They're not. Knowing which one applies to your trip—and how that choice affects your journey into the city—can save you a fair amount of confusion on arrival day.

This guide walks through **Fiumicino vs Ciampino**, the two airports serving Rome, so you can figure out which one you'll actually be using and what that means for getting into the city.

## Two Airports, Two Different Roles

Rome Fiumicino (FCO), officially named Leonardo da Vinci International Airport, is the city's main gateway. It handles the vast majority of international long-haul flights, including most transatlantic routes from North America, as well as the bulk of flights arriving from other major hubs around the world. If you're flying in on a full-service international carrier, especially from outside Europe, there's a strong chance you'll land at Fiumicino.

Rome Ciampino (CIA) is a smaller airport that primarily serves low-cost and charter carriers operating shorter regional and European routes. It's the airport many travelers pass through when flying in from other parts of Europe on a budget airline, or arriving on a charter flight rather than a scheduled international service.

Neither airport is "better" in an absolute sense. Which one matters to you depends entirely on which one your flight actually uses.

## How to Tell Which Airport You're Flying Into

This sounds obvious, but it trips people up more often than you'd expect—especially when booking through a third-party travel site that doesn't always make the airport code clear.

A few ways to check:

- Look at the three-letter airport code on your ticket or boarding pass: **FCO** means Fiumicino, **CIA** means Ciampino.
- Check your airline. Full-service international carriers overwhelmingly use Fiumicino. Several low-cost European carriers use Ciampino for at least some of their Rome routes, though not always exclusively.
- Don't assume based on the airline's home country alone—some carriers operate flights into both airports depending on the specific route.

If you're arranging any kind of pickup, whether that's a taxi, a shuttle, or a private [airport transfer](/airport-transfers), confirming the correct airport in advance matters. Sending a driver to the wrong airport, or turning up at the wrong terminal yourself expecting a different setup, is an easy and entirely avoidable mistake.

## Distance and Travel Time Into Rome

One of the clearest practical differences between the two airports is simply how far they are from central Rome.

Fiumicino sits roughly 35 km from the city center, and the trip into Rome typically takes approximately 40-50 minutes, depending on traffic. It's a straightforward but not especially short run, largely along a major highway corridor that can back up during peak periods.

Ciampino is considerably closer, at roughly 15 km from central Rome, with a typical journey of approximately 25-35 minutes, depending on traffic. In practice, that shorter distance can be offset somewhat by local road conditions on the Ciampino side, since the airport sits closer to residential and semi-urban areas rather than a dedicated highway link.

Either way, "depending on traffic" is doing real work in both of those estimates. Rome's roads can be unpredictable at almost any hour, and a transfer that takes 40 minutes on a quiet Tuesday morning can take considerably longer during a Friday evening rush or a holiday weekend.

## Terminal Size and Airport Experience

Fiumicino is a large, multi-terminal international airport. It has the scale you'd expect from a major hub: multiple terminals, a wide range of shops and services, and correspondingly longer walking distances between check-in, security, and gates. Navigating it comfortably usually means allowing more time than you might at a smaller airport, particularly for connections or unfamiliar layouts.

Ciampino is much more compact. It functions more like a large regional airport than an international hub, which generally means shorter walks and a simpler layout, but also fewer amenities and a more limited range of services once you're through security.

Neither approach is inherently a problem—they're just different experiences, and it helps to know which one you're walking into before you land.

## Getting From Each Airport Into the City

Both airports connect to Rome through a mix of trains, buses, taxis, and private transportation, but the specifics differ.

From Fiumicino, travelers generally have the widest range of options, including dedicated train services into the city center, official airport taxis, and pre-arranged private transfers. ## Connecting Flights and Multi-Airport Itineraries

Occasionally, travelers find themselves needing to use both airports on the same trip—flying into one and out of the other, for instance, or picking up a connecting charter flight after an international arrival. This is worth flagging early, because Fiumicino and Ciampino are not connected to each other by a quick shuttle or shared terminal system. Moving between them means a road journey of its own, on top of whatever transfer you've already planned into or out of Rome.

If your itinerary involves both airports, it's worth mapping out the full sequence in advance: arrival airport, transfer into (or through) Rome, and then transfer back out to the departure airport, with realistic time allowed for each leg. Treating the two airports as adjacent facilities, the way some multi-terminal hubs work, is a common and avoidable planning mistake.

## Signage, Terminal Layout, and What to Expect on Arrival

Fiumicino's scale means arriving passengers should expect clear but sometimes lengthy signage sequences directing them through passport control, baggage claim, and customs, particularly in the busier international terminals. It's generally well organized, but the sheer size of the building means it can take longer to physically walk from the aircraft to the arrivals area than a first-time visitor might expect.

Ciampino's smaller footprint generally makes wayfinding more intuitive simply because there's less ground to cover. That said, its facilities are more limited, and during busy arrival banks—when several low-cost flights land close together—the arrivals area can feel tight relative to a major hub, even though the walk itself is short.

## Which Airport Should Influence Your Planning?

If you already have your flight booked, the answer is simple: check your ticket for the airport code and plan around whichever one you're actually using. There's no need to compare the two once you know which applies to you.

If you're still choosing flights and have some flexibility, it's worth weighing a few factors. Fiumicino tends to offer more schedule options on full-service carriers and a wider range of onward connections, while Ciampino can sometimes offer more competitive fares on short-haul European routes, at the cost of a more limited terminal experience and fewer transport options into the city.

For travelers arriving with a lot of luggage, young children, or tight onward connections, some prefer to factor the airport's distance and transfer options into their flight choice rather than treating it purely as a formality. ## Final Thoughts

The Fiumicino vs Ciampino question isn't really about which airport is better—it's about knowing which one your itinerary actually involves and planning accordingly. Fiumicino is larger, farther from the city, and handles most international traffic. Ciampino is smaller, closer, and geared more toward low-cost and charter routes.

Once you know which airport you're using, the rest of your planning gets much easier: how long to budget for the transfer, what transport options are realistically available, and whether it's worth arranging a pickup in advance.
