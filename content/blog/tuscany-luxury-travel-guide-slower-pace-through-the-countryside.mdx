There's a version of a Tuscany trip that treats the region as a backdrop for a Florence stay — a day trip out, a day trip back, the countryside glimpsed through a car window between city nights. And there's a different version, where the countryside itself is the trip: a few nights based outside any city at all, in a farmhouse or a converted villa somewhere between the hill towns, with nowhere in particular to be from one morning to the next.

This guide is about the second version. It isn't built around Florence as a base with countryside excursions attached — it's about staying in the Tuscan countryside itself, choosing fewer towns rather than more, and letting the days move at whatever pace the region actually suggests.

## A Different Kind of Base

Most Tuscany trips default to a city — usually Florence, sometimes Siena or Pisa — with countryside visits treated as outings from there. There's nothing wrong with that approach, but it does mean every day in the countryside ends with a drive back to a hotel surrounded by other hotels, restaurants built for turnover, and the general noise of a city center.

Staying in the countryside instead changes the rhythm of a trip in a fairly fundamental way. Mornings start quietly, often with a view that has nothing urban in it at all. There's no need to drive out to reach the landscape, because you're already in it. And evenings don't require a return trip to anywhere — once the light starts to go, you're home for the day, wherever "home" happens to be for that stretch of the trip.

This kind of base usually means a farmhouse stay, a countryside estate, or a small property in or near one of the wine regions — Chianti between Florence and Siena, or further south around Montepulciano and Montalcino in the Val d'Orcia. None of these have their own major airport or train hub, which is really the point: reaching them comfortably means arranging a transfer rather than assuming easy public transport access, and getting around from there means having a car and driver on hand rather than relying on anything fixed.

## Fewer Towns, More Time in Each

A region-wide countryside trip works best when it resists the instinct to see everything. Tuscany has a long list of hill towns worth visiting — Siena, San Gimignano, Montepulciano, Montalcino, Lucca — and trying to fit all of them into a week, on top of actual relaxed time in the countryside, tends to turn the trip into a series of transfers rather than a slower stay.

A more deliberate approach picks two or three towns across several days, treating each as a half-day or full-day outing rather than a box to check, and leaves the remaining days without a town on the agenda at all. Those open days are where a countryside-based trip earns its name — time spent on the property itself, a slow drive with no destination beyond wherever looks interesting, or simply staying local to wherever you're based rather than driving anywhere.

## Unhurried Time in the Countryside Itself

The countryside between Tuscany's towns — Chianti, the Val d'Orcia, the hills around Montalcino — isn't just scenery to pass through on the way to somewhere else. It's arguably the main event of a slower trip like this, and it rewards time spent without a destination in mind: a longer lunch at a restaurant that happens to have a view, a wine tasting that runs well past the planned hour, a late afternoon spent simply sitting outside rather than moving on to the next stop.

This is difficult to arrange around a fixed itinerary or a rental car with somewhere to be by evening. It's much easier when the day genuinely has nowhere it needs to end up, and when the only plan is a loose sense of direction rather than a list of stops. A private driver who already knows the area can suggest a scenic road or a quiet village worth a detour, the kind of thing that rarely shows up on a map search but makes a countryside day feel considerably richer.

## Choosing a Vehicle for Rural Roads

A countryside-based trip usually means more time on narrower, less maintained roads than a city-based itinerary does, and vehicle choice matters a bit more here as a result. A [luxury sedan](/fleet/luxury-sedan) is comfortable for shorter countryside drives and city-adjacent stretches, but a [luxury SUV](/fleet/luxury-suv) tends to suit a longer countryside stay better — more clearance for rural and unpaved sections, more room for the extra layers of luggage that a multi-property stay tends to generate, and generally a smoother ride on the kind of roads this trip spends most of its time on. Groups traveling together across a longer countryside stay often do better in an [executive van](/fleet/executive-van) or [luxury van](/fleet/luxury-van), particularly if the itinerary includes several different lodging changes.

## A Realistic Multi-Day Shape

There's no single correct structure for a trip like this, but a loose shape that tends to work well looks something like: an arrival transfer directly into the countryside rather than a city stopover first, two or three days with no fixed plan beyond the property and perhaps one nearby village, one or two longer outings to a hill town further afield, and a departure transfer that doesn't require backtracking through a city unless your flight requires it.

## The Rhythm of the Seasons

A countryside-based stay also puts you closer to how the region actually changes through the year, which a city base tends to flatten out. Spring brings wildflowers and new green across the hills around Chianti and the Val d'Orcia. Summer slows everything down in the heat of the afternoon, with the best hours for being outside shifting earlier and later in the day. Harvest season in the autumn brings a different kind of energy to the wine regions, with activity in the vineyards that simply isn't visible from a Florence hotel room. None of this requires planning around specific events — it's more that the countryside itself feels different depending on when you're there, and a longer countryside stay lets you notice it in a way a single day trip rarely allows.

## Why Pace Matters More Than Itinerary

The difference between a rushed Tuscany trip and a genuinely luxurious one usually isn't the list of places visited — it's how much room each day has to breathe. A countryside-based stay, with fewer towns and more unstructured time, tends to deliver that kind of room more naturally than a city base with day trips attached, simply because there's less pressure to drive somewhere every single day.

If what you're after is a slower version of Tuscany — fewer stops, more time in the landscape itself, and days that don't revolve around getting back to a hotel in time for dinner — basing the trip in the countryside rather than in Florence is usually the single biggest decision that gets you there.
