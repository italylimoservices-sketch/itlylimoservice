Landing in Italy is the easy part. What happens between touchdown and the curb outside the terminal — passport control, baggage claim, customs, cash, connectivity — is where jet-lagged travelers lose the most time and make the most avoidable mistakes. This guide walks through that exact sequence, in order, so you know what to expect at Rome Fiumicino, Milan Malpensa, Venice Marco Polo, Naples, or any other Italian airport before you ever reach ground transportation.

This is strictly a terminal-side guide. It ends at the exit doors — for how to actually get into the city once you're there, see our separate guides on [airport transfers](/airport-transfers) and [chauffeur service](/chauffeur-service) in Italy.

## Deplaning and Following the Signage

Once the seatbelt sign goes off, resist the urge to sprint for the exit. Gather your documents — passport, boarding pass stub, any customs forms — into a jacket pocket or the top of your bag rather than burying them at the bottom of your carry-on. You'll need them again within minutes.

Italian airports use consistent, color-coded signage throughout the arrivals area, generally in Italian and English:

- **Passport control / Controllo Passaporti** — your first stop after leaving the jet bridge
- **Baggage claim / Ritiro Bagagli** — follow after clearing passport control
- **Customs / Dogana** — the green and red channels, just past baggage claim
- **Exit / Uscita** — leads to arrivals hall and ground transportation

Follow the overhead signs rather than the crowd — at larger hubs like Fiumicino and Malpensa, different terminals and even different piers within the same terminal can route arriving passengers through different corridors depending on whether your flight originated inside or outside the Schengen Area.

## Passport Control: EU Citizens vs. Non-EU Citizens

This is where the path splits, and it's the single biggest variable in how long your arrival takes.

### If You Hold an EU/EEA/Swiss Passport

You'll follow lanes marked for EU or "All Passports" and can generally use **e-gates** where available. Rome Fiumicino and Milan Malpensa both have automated e-gate lanes for EU and Italian passport holders (and some other eligible electronic passport holders) aged 12 and up — you scan your passport, look into a camera, and the gate opens in about 20 seconds if the system matches your face to your document. There's no officer interaction required for a normal, uneventful pass-through.

### If You Hold a Non-EU Passport (Including US, UK, Canadian, Australian)

You'll queue at lanes marked for "All Passports" or "Non-EU," where a border officer manually checks your passport. Be ready to answer a few brief, standard questions:

- Purpose of your trip (tourism, business, family visit)
- How long you plan to stay
- Where you're staying (having your hotel or accommodation address ready speeds this up)
- Sometimes: proof of onward travel or sufficient funds, though this is asked less often for typical tourist stays

Officers are processing a high volume of travelers quickly — short, direct answers move things along faster than a full itinerary recitation.

## The EU's Entry/Exit System (EES) — What's Actually in Effect Now

The EU's new **Entry/Exit System (EES)** — a digital border system that replaces manual passport stamping for non-EU, short-stay travelers with biometric registration (facial photo and, for most travelers, fingerprints) — began a phased rollout on 12 October 2025 and became **fully operational across the Schengen Area, including Italy, on 10 April 2026**. As of this writing it is live and enforced at Italian border crossings, with no blanket seasonal suspension in place, though individual airports retain limited authority to pause biometric capture for a few hours during extreme congestion.

What this means in practice for non-EU arrivals:

- **First-time registration** under EES takes longer than the old rubber-stamp process, since it involves a passport scan, fingerprints (for visa-exempt travelers), a facial photo, and a brief officer check. Budget extra time, especially at busy hubs like Fiumicino and Malpensa during peak arrival banks.
- **Return visits** within the system's validity window are faster — the kiosk or officer only needs to verify your identity against the record already on file, not capture it from scratch.
- **Self-service kiosks**, where available, are generally faster than staffed desks for eligible travelers and are worth using if you see one with a short line.
- EU/EEA/Swiss citizens are not part of EES and continue to use e-gates or the EU lane as before.

Because EES enforcement details, kiosk availability, and airport-specific procedures continue to be refined, treat any of the above as the general framework rather than a guarantee of a specific wait time — and build a comfortable connection buffer into your itinerary if you're arriving on an international flight with a tight onward connection.

## Baggage Claim

After clearing passport control, overhead screens in the baggage hall list your flight number next to a carousel (nastro) number. This is also where knowing your airport matters:

- At **[Rome Fiumicino](/airport-transfers/rome-fiumicino)**, arrivals are split across multiple terminals depending on airline and whether your flight was Schengen or non-Schengen, so the carousel assignment can be a longer walk than expected.
- At **[Milan Malpensa](/airport-transfers/milan-malpensa)**, Terminal 1 and Terminal 2 are physically separate buildings — confirm which terminal your flight lands at before you plan to meet anyone.
- At **[Venice Marco Polo](/airport-transfers/venice-marco-polo)** and **[Naples](/airport-transfers/naples)**, the arrivals hall is more compact and carousels are easier to locate on sight.

Double check the carousel screen periodically — assignments occasionally change if a flight arrives early or a carousel is reassigned.

### If Your Luggage Doesn't Show Up

Go straight to the airline's or ground handler's **baggage service desk**, usually located right at the baggage claim area before you exit through customs, and file a **Property Irregularity Report (PIR)**. A few points worth knowing:

- The PIR is generated on an international tracing system that most airlines and handlers share, so it's searchable across airports, not just locally.
- You'll need your bag tag receipt, boarding pass, and a description of the bag — take a photo of your checked bag before you fly if you don't already.
- Get the PIR reference number before you leave the airport. Most airlines will not process a delayed or lost bag claim without it.
- Ask for a written note of what happens next: many airlines will deliver a delayed bag to your accommodation once it's located, so make sure the address on file is correct.
- A bag is generally treated as "delayed" for the first few weeks; only after an extended period without resolution does it shift to a formal "lost" claim with different compensation rules. Ask the desk agent for the specific timeline that applies to your airline and ticket type.

Don't skip this step even if you're exhausted and just want to leave — filing it later from your hotel is significantly harder and, with some airlines, not accepted at all.

## Customs: Green Channel vs. Red Channel

Past baggage claim, before the exit doors, you'll pass through customs. There are two channels, and you choose one — there's no desk to check in at:

- **Green channel ("Nothing to Declare")** — for travelers carrying only personal belongings and goods within the standard duty-free allowances.
- **Red channel ("Goods to Declare")** — for anyone carrying goods over the allowance, restricted items, large sums of cash, or anything they're unsure about.

### General EU Duty-Free Allowances for Arrivals from Outside the EU

If you're arriving from a non-EU country, the standard EU-wide allowances for personal, non-commercial goods are approximately:

- **Tobacco:** 200 cigarettes, or 100 cigarillos, or 50 cigars, or 250g of tobacco (travelers under 17 get no tobacco or alcohol allowance)
- **Alcohol:** 1 liter of spirits over 22% ABV, or 2 liters of fortified/sparkling wine under 22% ABV, plus separately 4 liters of still wine and 16 liters of beer
- **Other goods:** up to roughly €300 per traveler (€430 for air and sea travelers) in value

These figures can shift and vary somewhat by category, so treat them as general guidance rather than a final word — if you're carrying anything close to these limits, confirm current thresholds or simply use the red channel.

**Cash declaration:** carrying €10,000 or more in cash (or equivalent, including combined currencies) into the EU requires a declaration, regardless of which channel you'd otherwise use. This is a reporting requirement, not a tax, but it's strictly enforced and worth taking seriously.

If you're traveling only between EU/Schengen countries with nothing unusual to declare, most Italian airports won't route you through a formal customs check at all — you'll simply exit toward arrivals.

## Currency Exchange and ATMs at the Airport

You'll pass exchange kiosks and ATMs before you reach the exit. A few practical points:

- **Airport currency exchange counters typically offer the worst rates you'll find anywhere on your trip**, padded with fees and poor spreads. If you need a small amount of euros in hand immediately — for a coffee, a luggage cart, or a tip — exchange or withdraw only a modest amount here.
- **The first ATMs you encounter in arrivals halls are often independent, non-bank machines** (some well-known brands have a reputation for poor rates and aggressive currency conversion prompts). Where possible, wait until you spot a **machine branded by an actual Italian bank** (for example, Intesa Sanpaolo, UniCredit, or similar) for a better rate — these are often located slightly further into the arrivals hall or just outside it.
- **When an ATM or card terminal asks whether to charge you in euros or your home currency, always choose euros.** Accepting the home-currency option triggers dynamic currency conversion (DCC), which applies a marked-up exchange rate under the guise of convenience.
- A quick heads-up to your bank or card issuer before departure prevents your card from being flagged or frozen on first use abroad.

You don't need much cash on arrival — enough to cover incidentals for the first few hours is plenty, since most hotels, restaurants, and transport options in Italy's major cities accept cards widely.

## Getting a Local SIM or eSIM

Connectivity matters immediately — for maps, translation, and coordinating with your accommodation or driver. You have three practical options, roughly in order of convenience:

1. **eSIM purchased before departure** — activates automatically on landing (or as soon as you enable it), no physical shop visit or SIM tray needed. This is the fastest option if your phone supports eSIM.
2. **SIM kiosks or vending machines in the arrivals hall** — most major Italian airports have a mobile carrier desk or automated kiosk (TIM, Vodafone, WindTre are the main Italian carriers) where you can buy a physical prepaid SIM with a data plan, generally requiring your passport for registration.
3. **Airport Wi-Fi** — free but limited, useful only to get you through the terminal until you sort out a SIM or eSIM in the city.

If connectivity matters from the moment you land — for example, to reach a pre-booked driver — arranging an eSIM before you fly removes one more thing to deal with in a jet-lagged first hour.

## Finding Ground Transportation and Exiting the Terminal

Once you're through the exit doors, you're in the arrivals hall or directly outside it, where signage typically directs you toward:

- **Taxis** (marked ranks, usually with clear signage and, at major airports, fixed-rate zones to city centers)
- **Private transfers and chauffeur pickup** (often a designated meeting point or arrivals board area — confirm this with whoever arranged your pickup beforehand)
- **Trains and buses** (usually requiring a short walk or shuttle from the terminal, more common at Fiumicino and Malpensa than at smaller airports)
- **Rental car counters and shuttles**

Deciding how to get from the airport to your hotel is the next step after arrival — and it's covered in depth in our dedicated guides for [Rome](/destinations/rome), [Milan](/destinations/milan), and each airport's [transfer options](/airport-transfers), rather than here. If a driver is meeting you, look for your name on an arrivals board or confirm the exact meeting point in advance — most pickups happen just outside the terminal doors or at a marked short-term parking or pickup zone rather than inside the baggage hall.

## Quick-Reference: The Arrival Sequence

| Step | What Happens | Approx. Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Deplane | Follow signage toward Passport Control | 5–10 min |
| 2. Passport control | EU: e-gates; Non-EU: officer check + EES registration if first-time | 5–45 min (highly variable) |
| 3. Baggage claim | Locate carousel via screens; file PIR if bag is missing | 15–30 min |
| 4. Customs | Choose green (nothing to declare) or red (goods to declare) channel | 1–5 min (usually no stop) |
| 5. Currency/SIM | Withdraw minimal cash from a bank ATM; activate eSIM or buy local SIM | 5–15 min |
| 6. Exit terminal | Follow signage to taxis, private transfer, train, or rental car | 5–10 min |

Actual times vary enormously by airport, time of day, and — since April 2026 — by how EES processing is running that day, so treat this as a sequence guide rather than a fixed schedule.

## First-Hour Tips

- **Don't rush passport control.** Trying to jump lanes or rush an officer's questions almost always slows things down rather than speeding them up.
- **Keep your passport and any customs paperwork accessible** until you're fully through customs — you may be asked for it more than once.
- **Don't over-exchange currency at the airport.** A small amount for immediate needs is enough; better rates wait for you at a proper bank ATM.
- **File a PIR immediately** if your bag doesn't arrive — not after you've left the airport.
- **Confirm your pickup meeting point before you land**, whether it's a taxi rank, a private driver, or a rental counter, so you're not searching for signage while jet-lagged.
- **Give yourself buffer time on connections**, particularly at Rome Fiumicino and Milan Malpensa, where EES processing can add unpredictable time to non-EU passport checks during peak arrival banks.

Once you're through the terminal doors, the arrival process is technically over — what comes next is simply choosing how you get into the city.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need to do anything special for the EU's Entry/Exit System if I'm an EU citizen?**
No. EES applies to non-EU, short-stay travelers. EU, EEA, and Swiss citizens continue to use the EU lanes or e-gates as before, with no biometric registration required.

**How long does passport control usually take for non-EU passport holders in Italy?**
It varies significantly by airport, time of day, and whether it's your first EES registration or a return visit. First-time registration takes longer since it includes a passport scan, photo, and fingerprints; returning travelers already enrolled move faster. Peak arrival banks at Rome Fiumicino and Milan Malpensa can see longer waits than smaller airports.

**What happens if my checked bag doesn't arrive?**
Go to the airline or ground handler's baggage service desk before leaving the airport and file a Property Irregularity Report (PIR). Keep the PIR reference number — you'll need it to track the bag or file any compensation claim, and most airlines require it before they'll process a delayed or lost bag case.

**Which customs channel should I use if I'm not sure whether I'm within the allowance?**
Use the red ("goods to declare") channel. It's always the safer choice if there's any doubt, and customs officers can tell you on the spot whether duty applies.

**Should I exchange money at the airport?**
Only exchange or withdraw a small amount at the airport for immediate needs — rates and fees at airport exchange counters and the first ATMs you encounter in arrivals are typically worse than a bank-branded ATM found a bit further along, or in the city itself.

**Can I buy a SIM card at the airport, or should I get an eSIM before I fly?**
Both work. Major Italian airports have carrier kiosks or vending machines for physical prepaid SIMs. An eSIM purchased before departure is generally more convenient since it can activate the moment you land without a shop visit.

**Is the EU's Entry/Exit System fully running in Italy right now?**
Yes — as of this writing, EES has been fully operational at Italian border crossing points, including Rome Fiumicino and Milan Malpensa, since 10 April 2026, following a phased rollout that began in October 2025. Airports retain limited flexibility to pause biometric capture briefly during extreme congestion, so processing experiences can still vary.

**What's the cash declaration threshold when entering Italy from outside the EU?**
If you're carrying €10,000 or more in cash or equivalent, you must declare it to customs on arrival. This is a reporting requirement rather than a tax, but it applies regardless of which customs channel you'd otherwise use.
