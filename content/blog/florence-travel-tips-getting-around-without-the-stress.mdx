Florence has a way of making travelers overthink their transportation. Maybe it's the reputation Italian cities have for chaotic traffic, or the fact that so many blog posts about Italy dwell on ZTL fines and confusing bus routes. In reality, **getting around Florence** is one of the more straightforward parts of visiting the city — you just need to know which option fits which situation, so you're not standing on a street corner weighing your options with tired feet.

This guide covers the practical basics of moving around Florence: when to walk, when a taxi makes sense, when a private transfer is worth arranging, and a brief note on driving yourself, without turning into an exhaustive rulebook.

## Florence Is a Walking City First

Florence's historic center is compact, and this is genuinely the single most useful thing to know before you arrive. Many of the city's major sights — the Duomo, the Uffizi, Ponte Vecchio, Piazza della Signoria — sit within a relatively small area that most visitors can cross on foot in well under half an hour. If your hotel is anywhere near the center, you may find you barely need any other form of transport for day-to-day sightseeing.

Walking also happens to be the best way to actually experience Florence. The city rewards wandering — a side street off the main tourist path, a quiet piazza, a small shop you weren't looking for. None of that is available to you from inside a car.

That said, walking has its limits. Cobblestone streets, narrow sidewalks, and the general fatigue of a day spent on your feet add up, especially if you're covering ground between multiple sights or carrying anything heavier than a day bag. It's worth pacing yourself rather than assuming you'll want to walk everywhere for an entire stay.

## Taxis for When Walking Doesn't Make Sense

Taxis are a reliable fallback for trips that are too far to walk comfortably, or for times when you're simply too tired to make the effort. Florence has taxi ranks at key points around the city, including near the main train station and major squares, and they're a reasonably straightforward option if you don't mind flagging one down or joining a short queue.

Taxis make particular sense for trips outside the historic center, late-evening returns to your hotel, or any time you're carrying luggage or shopping bags that make walking impractical.

## Private Transfer for Predictability

A private chauffeur or transfer service is worth considering any time you want the trip itself to be one less thing to think about — arriving from the airport, heading to a dinner reservation across town, or planning a day trip outside the city. Rather than working out a taxi or navigating public transport on the spot, a pre-arranged car means the pickup time, location, and destination are already settled.

This tends to matter most for families, groups with a lot of luggage, business travelers with a schedule to keep, or anyone who'd simply rather not spend part of their trip figuring out transportation logistics. An [hourly chauffeur](/hourly-chauffeur) can also be useful if you want a driver available across a few stops in a single day, rather than booking separate trips each time.

## A Brief Note on Driving Yourself

If you're renting a car for a wider Italian itinerary, it's worth knowing that Florence's historic center, like many Italian city centers, has restricted traffic zones that limit which vehicles can enter certain streets. The general idea is simple: some areas of the center aren't meant for casual driving, and cameras can record vehicles that enter without authorization. This isn't something that should worry you if you're not driving in the historic core yourself — taxis, transfers, and local drivers are familiar with the restrictions — but if you do plan to self-drive into central Florence, it's worth checking the specifics for your route before you go, rather than working it out by trial and error.

For most visitors focused on Florence itself rather than a longer countryside drive, renting a car isn't necessary at all. It becomes more relevant if your plans extend into the Tuscan countryside, where a car — or a private driver — opens up villages and back roads that aren't well served by public transport.

## Getting In From the Airport or Train Station

However you plan to get around once you're settled in Florence, the first leg of the trip is worth a little separate thought. Arriving at [Florence Airport](/airport-transfers/florence) puts you roughly 5 km from the city center, typically a 15 to 20 minute drive depending on traffic, while arriving by train at Santa Maria Novella drops you directly in the historic core. Both are short journeys, but "short" and "simple" aren't always the same thing once luggage, fatigue, and an unfamiliar city are part of the equation. Deciding how you'll cover that first stretch before you arrive means one less thing to figure out on the day.

## Evenings and Late Returns

Daytime logistics in Florence tend to sort themselves out fairly easily, since the historic center is busy, well-lit, and easy to navigate on foot. Evenings are a little different. After a late dinner, walking back to your hotel is still perfectly reasonable in most cases, but if your accommodation is a longer walk away, or if you're simply ready to be done moving for the day, a taxi or a pre-arranged transfer takes the guesswork out of an otherwise pleasant evening. This is also where planning ahead helps most: agreeing on a pickup time before you sit down for dinner means you're not standing outside a restaurant at 10 p.m. trying to flag down a cab.

## Matching the Option to the Moment

A lot of the "stress" travelers associate with getting around Florence comes down to using the wrong tool for a given trip — walking a distance that's really too far for tired feet, or booking a car for a five-minute hop that would have been a pleasant stroll. A rough guide:

- **Short distances within the historic center:** walking, almost always
- **Longer distances, luggage, or fatigue:** taxi
- **Airport transfers, day trips, multi-stop days, or a fixed schedule:** private transfer
- **Countryside exploration beyond Florence:** rental car or private driver

None of these choices is locked in for your whole trip. Most visitors end up mixing all three depending on the day.

## Reducing Stress Before You Even Arrive

A little planning goes a long way toward keeping Florence's logistics simple. Knowing roughly where your hotel sits relative to the main sights, having a sense of how you'll get in from [Florence Airport](/airport-transfers/florence) or the train station, and deciding in advance whether any day trips will need a car all help you avoid last-minute scrambling once you're actually in the city and would rather be enjoying it.

## Final Thoughts

Getting around Florence doesn't have to be complicated, and it generally isn't, as long as you match the transportation to the trip rather than defaulting to whichever option feels familiar from home. Walk when you can, take a taxi when your feet or your schedule say otherwise, and consider arranging a [private chauffeur service](/chauffeur-service) for the parts of your visit where predictability matters more than flexibility. The goal isn't to plan every movement down to the minute — it's to remove just enough guesswork that getting around becomes background noise rather than something you have to think about at all.
