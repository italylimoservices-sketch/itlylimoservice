Naples packs a UNESCO-listed historic center, a seafront lined with castles, and a hilltop panorama into a city that's considerably more compact than it feels from inside a taxi stuck in its traffic. The trouble isn't distance so much as accessibility — the oldest streets weren't built for cars, one of the best viewpoints sits up a steep hill, and the waterfront runs along a completely different axis from the historic core. A private chauffeur doesn't shrink the city, but it does mean the gaps between the best places to visit in Naples get covered by car rather than by guesswork.

Here's a look at Naples' major landmarks grouped the way they actually sit on the map, along with what to expect from vehicle access in each part of the city.

## Naples Is Dense, Not Spread Out

Compared with Rome or Florence, Naples covers a smaller footprint, but its layout is less forgiving to navigate. The historic center is a tight grid of narrow streets laid out centuries before cars existed, the waterfront curves along the bay well outside that grid, and the Vomero hill overlooks everything from several hundred feet above sea level. None of these areas connect by a short, obvious walk, and the traffic between them can be some of the most intense of any Italian city on a typical itinerary.

A chauffeur familiar with Naples reads this layout in a way a first-time visitor can't, choosing routes around the historic center's restrictions rather than into them, and getting up to the Vomero by road instead of relying on a long uphill walk or a crowded funicular queue.

## The Historic Center and Spaccanapoli

Naples' historic center is one of the largest in Europe and carries UNESCO World Heritage status, and its narrow streets are still busy with the ordinary business of the city rather than existing solely for visitors — laundry strung overhead, small shops, and scooters navigating alleys barely wide enough for them. Spaccanapoli, the long straight street that cuts through the heart of the old center, is the easiest way to get oriented here, since it runs in a single line through several of the neighborhood's key corners rather than winding unpredictably.

Vehicle access in this part of the city is limited by the streets themselves as much as by any formal restriction — many are simply too narrow, too crowded, or too pedestrian-heavy for a car to pass through comfortably. A chauffeur will typically drop you at the nearest practical point at the edge of the historic center and arrange a pickup at another accessible spot once you're done exploring on foot, rather than trying to weave a vehicle into the grid itself.

## Naples Cathedral (Duomo di San Gennaro)

A short walk off Spaccanapoli, Naples Cathedral, dedicated to the city's patron saint, San Gennaro, sits within the same dense historic grid. Because it's embedded in the old center rather than facing a large open piazza the way some Italian cathedrals do, reaching it involves the same narrow streets as the rest of the district, and the same logic applies to getting there by car: a nearby drop-off point rather than a direct approach to the door.

Given its position, the Duomo is easiest to fold into the same stretch of a sightseeing day as Spaccanapoli and the surrounding historic center, rather than treating it as a separate stop that needs its own transfer.

## Castel dell'Ovo and the Waterfront

On the opposite side of the city's layout from the historic center, Castel dell'Ovo sits on a small outcrop along the Naples waterfront, connected to the mainland by a short causeway. It's one of the more photogenic stops in the city, with the castle in the foreground and the bay, and often Vesuvius, in the background. The Lungomare, Naples' seafront promenade, runs along this stretch and offers a noticeably different atmosphere from the historic center — open, breezy, and far easier for a vehicle to approach.

Castel Nuovo, the city's other major castle, sits a bit further along toward the port and the municipal square, and pairs naturally with a waterfront stop since both sit outside the tightest part of the old center. Because vehicle access along the Lungomare is considerably more straightforward than in the historic core, this stretch of the city suits a chauffeur handling drop-offs and pickups directly rather than at a distance.

## The National Archaeological Museum

North of the historic center, the National Archaeological Museum holds one of Italy's most significant collections, including a substantial number of artifacts recovered from Pompeii and Herculaneum. For visitors planning a day trip to either site, or who've already been, the museum adds context that's hard to get standing among the ruins themselves. It sits close enough to the historic center to work as an add-on to a day built around Spaccanapoli and the Duomo, though it's worth treating as its own stop rather than a quick pass-through, since the collection is large enough to fill a genuine chunk of a morning or afternoon.

## Vomero Hill and Certosa di San Martino

The Vomero hill rises well above the rest of the city, and the view from up here, particularly around the Certosa di San Martino, takes in the historic center, the bay, and Vesuvius in a single sweep. It's comparable in spirit to hillside viewpoints in other Italian cities, but the climb is steeper and a walk up considerably less practical, which is why most visitors reach the Vomero by funicular, taxi, or private car rather than on foot.

A chauffeur can take you directly up to the Vomero and be waiting when you're ready to head back down, which matters more here than almost anywhere else on this list — there's no steady flow of taxis waiting at a hilltop viewpoint the way there is around the historic center or the waterfront.

## Grouping the Best Places to Visit in Naples by Location

The most common mistake in planning a Naples sightseeing day is listing landmarks in order of fame rather than geography. Spaccanapoli, the Duomo, and the historic center sit together in the old core; Castel dell'Ovo, the Lungomare, and Castel Nuovo sit along the waterfront on a different side of the city; the National Archaeological Museum sits just north of the historic center; and the Vomero rises above all of it. Bouncing between the waterfront and the Vomero in the middle of a morning built around the historic center adds driving time, and driving time in Naples, given the traffic, is worth minimizing rather than accepting as unavoidable.

A chauffeur who knows the city can sequence these stops so the day moves in a sensible direction rather than crossing back and forth, which becomes more valuable the more of these landmarks you're trying to fit into a single visit.
