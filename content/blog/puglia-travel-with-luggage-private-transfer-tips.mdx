Luggage is one of those things travelers underthink until it's actually a problem. In most of Italy, a few extra bags are a minor annoyance. In Puglia's historic town centers, they can be the difference between a smooth arrival and a long, awkward walk dragging a suitcase over cobblestones.

## The Core Issue: Puglia's Old Towns Weren't Built for Cars

Alberobello's trulli district and Ostuni's whitewashed old town are the two places this comes up most, and Lecce's baroque center isn't far behind. These neighborhoods predate the automobile by centuries, and it shows — streets narrow to the width of a person with shopping bags, stairs appear where you'd expect a ramp, and vehicle access is often restricted outright, whether by width, by local traffic rules, or simply by the physical impossibility of turning a car through the lanes.

What this means in practice is that a vehicle, private or rented, usually can't get you all the way to your hotel door in these towns. There's a walk involved, and the distance of that walk varies a lot depending on exactly where you're staying and which entrance to the old town a vehicle can actually reach. No fixed figure applies across the board — it depends on the specific accommodation and the specific town — but the honest planning assumption is "further than you'd like while carrying bags," not "right outside."

## Where Luggage Problems Actually Start

A few patterns show up often enough to be worth naming directly:

**Cobblestones and uneven paving.** Wheeled suitcases do not glide over old stone the way they do over a smooth airport floor. A hard-shell case with small wheels can turn a five-minute walk into a genuinely tiring one.

**Steps without warning.** Older buildings in Puglia's historic centers were rarely designed with step-free access in mind, and a route that looks flat on a map can still involve a short staircase or an uneven threshold.

**Restricted zones that aren't obvious until you're there.** Some old-town areas limit vehicle access by permit, by time of day, or simply by width, and the restriction isn't always signposted in a way a visitor would catch before driving straight into a lane they can't turn around in.

**Multiple stops in one day.** A day that includes Alberobello in the morning and Ostuni in the afternoon means loading and unloading luggage more than once, which multiplies any friction from the first three points.

## How a Private Transfer Changes the Equation

None of this disappears with a private driver — the streets are still narrow, and the laws of physics still apply to a rolling suitcase on cobblestone. What changes is who's managing the logistics around it.

A driver who knows a given town can get you to the closest realistic drop-off point rather than guessing, which saves the worst of the unnecessary walking. Luggage gets handled at the vehicle rather than left for you to wrestle out of a trunk while also managing kids, directions, or a hotel check-in call. And if a day involves more than one town, the vehicle and your bags move with you as a single unit instead of being re-packed into a different rental or taxi at each stop.

## Choosing the Right Vehicle for Luggage, Not Just People

Vehicle choice is often decided by passenger count alone, which is a mistake when luggage is a real factor. A [luxury sedan](/fleet/luxury-sedan) or [executive sedan](/fleet/executive-sedan) suits a couple or small group with reasonable luggage, but the math changes fast once oversized bags, golf clubs, or extra shopping enter the picture. A [luxury SUV](/fleet/luxury-suv) carries up to five passengers and four suitcases, which tends to be the more comfortable choice for a family or small group bringing more than the minimum. For a larger group or anyone traveling with noticeably more luggage than the headcount implies, an [executive van](/fleet/executive-van) or [luxury van](/fleet/luxury-van) — each seating up to seven and holding up to six suitcases — leaves real room rather than a tight squeeze.

The practical move is to count bags honestly when requesting a quote, not just people. "Four passengers" and "four passengers with six checked bags and two carry-ons" are different requests, and naming the second one accurately is what gets the right vehicle suggested in the first place.

## Planning a Multi-Stop Touring Day With Luggage in Mind

A day that strings together more than one Puglia town — Alberobello and Ostuni, say, or a countryside stop between Bari and Lecce — runs more smoothly with a bit of upfront thought about where the bags actually live during the day.

- If you're touring with full luggage rather than just day bags, flag that clearly when booking so the vehicle has room for both passengers and cases
- Decide in advance whether luggage stays in the vehicle during a town visit or needs to come with you, and say so in your booking notes if it affects timing
- For a day with several stops, naming the sequence clearly — not just a single pickup and destination — helps the driver plan the realistic drop-off point at each one
- If you're arriving with more bags than a quick weekend would suggest, mention it even if the passenger count looks modest

## Arriving With Luggage Through Bari or Brindisi

Puglia's two main airport gateways, Bari and Brindisi, are where most visitors first take on a full set of luggage for the trip. A pre-arranged transfer means the right vehicle is already sized for your bags before you land, rather than discovering at the rental counter or taxi rank that what you brought doesn't comfortably fit what's available. ## Multi-Day Trips: Luggage That Changes Hotels Too

A one-night stop is one thing. A multi-day Puglia itinerary that moves between a few different towns is another, because it usually means luggage gets fully loaded and unloaded at each new hotel rather than sitting in a trunk for the whole trip. That's worth planning around rather than discovering on day two.

A few things help on a multi-day trip specifically:

- Pack in a way that makes partial access easy — a smaller bag for a one-night stop inside a car-free old town, with the rest left in the vehicle, can save a lot of unnecessary carrying
- If an itinerary includes a night actually inside a historic center like Alberobello or Ostuni, confirm with your accommodation how far the nearest realistic vehicle drop-off is, since it varies property to property
- Mention any hotel changes mid-trip in your booking request so the driver knows luggage needs to be unloaded and reloaded more than once, not just at the start and end

None of this is complicated, but it's the kind of detail that's much easier to handle when it's known in advance than when it's improvised at a curb.

## A Word on Packing Light for Puglia Specifically

Puglia doesn't call for anything unusual in terms of packing, but the cobblestones-and-steps reality of its old towns is a reasonable argument for erring toward fewer, more manageable bags rather than maximizing what you bring. A hard-shell case with sturdy wheels still beats a soft duffel on rough paving, and anyone planning to walk a meaningful distance into a historic center is better off with one bag they can carry up a short flight of stairs than two they have to make two trips for. This isn't a verified rule, just a practical observation that tends to hold up across Puglia's old towns in particular.

## The Short Version

Puglia's historic centers are worth the walk — Alberobello and Ostuni in particular are best experienced on foot once you're inside them — but the walk is easier without a suitcase fighting you the whole way. Naming your luggage honestly when you book, choosing a vehicle with real room rather than the tightest fit, and letting a driver who knows the towns handle the drop-off point are the three things that make the most difference.
