Most airport guides stop at the city the airport is named after. That's fine if you're spending your whole trip in Rome or Venice proper — but a huge share of travelers land at one of these hubs specifically because it's the closest gate to somewhere else: Tuscany, the Amalfi Coast, Lake Como, the Dolomites. That onward leg is a different logistical problem than the airport-to-downtown hop, and it's the one most guides skip.

The reason it's skipped is that it's genuinely messier. Airport-to-city-center connections are usually a single train or a short, well-signed taxi ride. Airport-to-secondary-destination connections are longer, often involve at least one change of transport, and in several cases have no direct public transport option at all. This guide covers those longer connections specifically — organized by the airport you're likely to land at, with the realistic ways travelers actually make each trip.

## Rome Fiumicino and Ciampino: gateway to central Italy and the south

Rome's two airports handle the country's busiest arrivals, and [Rome Fiumicino](/airport-transfers/rome-fiumicino) in particular functions as a launch point well beyond the capital. Ciampino, smaller and served mostly by low-cost carriers, has thinner onward connections and fewer rail options, so most of what follows leans toward Fiumicino unless noted.

### Onward to Florence and Tuscany

Florence sits roughly 235 km north of Fiumicino, and the direct train option is more limited than most travelers expect: only one or two Frecciarossa high-speed services a day run straight from the airport to Florence, in around 2 hours 19 minutes. Miss those, and the standard route is Leonardo Express into Roma Termini, then a separate high-speed train onward — workable, but it turns a single journey into two bookings and a station transfer with luggage.

Driving covers the same ground in roughly 2.5 to 3 hours door to door, and a private transfer follows that same road time without the Termini changeover, which is the practical appeal for travelers arriving with jet lag or heavy luggage. If your onward plans include wider Tuscany touring — hill towns, wineries, multiple bases — renting a car at the airport is also a reasonable call, since Tuscany rewards flexible, self-driven exploring far more than Florence itself does. Fixed-schedule train travel makes less sense once you're hopping between small towns with infrequent service.

### Onward to Naples, Sorrento, and the Amalfi Coast

Naples is the better-served of Rome's southern connections. High-speed trains from Roma Termini reach Napoli Centrale in about an hour, and the total airport-to-Naples drive covers roughly 225–245 km in around 2 hours 15 to 2.5 hours by road via the A1 motorway. Either way works reasonably well for Naples itself.

The calculation changes once Sorrento or the Amalfi Coast is the actual destination. There's no direct train to either — you'd need Rome to Naples by rail, then a change to the Circumvesuviana line or a bus for the final stretch, plus the notoriously slow, winding coastal roads for the last few kilometers into Positano or Amalfi. Total driving distance from Fiumicino to Sorrento runs around 280 km, roughly 3 hours by direct road, and a direct [private transfer from Rome to Sorrento](/routes/rome-to-sorrento) or a [Rome to Amalfi Coast route](/routes/rome-to-amalfi-coast) is genuinely the more practical choice here — not because public transport is impossible, but because a same-day multi-leg train-and-bus combination with luggage over that distance is a long day for a coastline whose whole appeal is arriving relaxed. Self-driving is the one option worth flagging as a poor fit: the Amalfi coastal road is narrow, congested in high season, and stressful for first-time visitors managing an unfamiliar rental.

### Onward to Civitavecchia cruise port

If your trip starts or ends with a Mediterranean cruise, Civitavecchia is the relevant connection, and it's the shortest one on this list — about 66–71 km from Fiumicino, covered in roughly 45–50 minutes by road. There's no dedicated airport-to-port train; the practical options are a direct road transfer or a train into Rome followed by a regional service back out to Civitavecchia, which adds time without much benefit given how short the direct drive is. For cruise passengers with an embarkation deadline, a scheduled private transfer removes the single biggest risk factor: missing the ship.

## Milan Malpensa and Linate: gateway to the lakes and the north

Milan's two airports split the workload differently than Rome's. [Malpensa](/airport-transfers/milan-malpensa) is the larger international hub, roughly 45 km from central Milan, while Linate sits much closer to the city and handles more domestic and short-haul European traffic. For onward travel to the lakes, Malpensa's location actually works in travelers' favor — it sits north and west of the city, on the same side as Lake Maggiore and reasonably positioned for Lake Como too.

### Onward to Lake Como

[Lake Como](/destinations/lake-como) is about 50 km from Malpensa. Driving takes 45 minutes to 1.5 hours depending on traffic, which fluctuates more than the distance suggests given how congested the roads around Milan and Como town can get on weekends. Rail is workable but indirect: there's no direct train from the airport, so the standard route runs via Saronno to Como San Giovanni, taking roughly 1 hour 15 to 1 hour 20 minutes including the connection.

For travelers heading straight to a specific lakeside town rather than Como town itself — Bellagio, Varenna, Menaggio — the train option gets weaker still, since those require an additional bus or ferry leg from the station. A direct [private transfer from Milan to Lake Como](/routes/milan-to-lake-como) skips both the Saronno change and the onward local connection, which is why it's the default recommendation for anyone not renting a car. Renting is also a solid option if the lake trip includes hopping between multiple villages, since the lake's shoreline roads and ferry schedules are easier to work around with your own vehicle.

### Onward to Lake Maggiore

Lake Maggiore's main resort town, Stresa, sits closer to Malpensa than Como does — around 45–60 km, with driving times in the 45-minute to 1-hour range. A direct bus runs from Malpensa Terminal 1 to Stresa roughly every four hours, taking about 1 hour 10 minutes, which makes it slightly more convenient by public transport than the Como run, provided the bus schedule lines up with your flight.

### Onward to northern Italy generally

Malpensa's position also makes it a reasonable gateway for onward trips toward Turin, the Piedmont wine country, or further into the Alps — none of which have a single standardized route, so the right call depends heavily on the specific target. As a general rule: anywhere with a direct high-speed rail link from Milano Centrale is well served by train once you've transferred into the city; anywhere off that spine is a better fit for a car, either self-driven or with a private driver for longer point-to-point legs.

## Venice Marco Polo: gateway to Verona, Padua, and the Dolomites

[Venice Marco Polo](/airport-transfers/venice-marco-polo) has a detail that changes every onward calculation here: the airport itself has no train station. Every rail journey out of Marco Polo starts with a bus or water connection into Venezia Mestre (about 20 minutes by ATVO express bus) or the historic center, before any train departs. That extra step is worth planning around, since it turns a "direct train" search into something that isn't quite as direct as it looks.

### Onward to Verona

[Verona](/destinations/verona) is about 105 km west of the airport. Once you've reached Mestre, direct trains to Verona run frequently and take around 1 hour 15 to 1 hour 20 minutes at the fastest. Driving the same route takes roughly 1 hour 20 minutes to 1 hour 40 minutes. Given the Mestre transfer, total door-to-door time by public transport and by road end up fairly close, which makes this one of the more balanced choices on this list — a private transfer mainly buys back the bus-to-train changeover rather than saving significant road time.

### Onward to Padua

Padua — actually one of the shortest and easiest onward hops from Venice's airport — sits only around 40 km away. From Mestre, trains reach Padua in as little as 28–30 minutes, and frequent departures make this one of the few onward routes on this list where public transport comfortably beats arranging a private car on convenience alone, assuming you don't mind the initial bus transfer.

### Onward to the Dolomites

The Dolomites region, using Cortina d'Ampezzo as a reference point, is roughly 150 km from Marco Polo. Driving takes approximately 1 hour 45 minutes in ideal conditions, though 2 to 2.5 hours is more realistic once you account for the mountain roads near Belluno and seasonal traffic. There's no train service into the high Dolomites — the rail network doesn't reach that far into the mountains — so the realistic choices are a long-distance bus (several operators run seasonal routes), a direct private transfer, or a rental car. For anyone carrying ski equipment or arriving outside the limited bus schedule windows, a direct transfer or self-drive is the only practical option; this is one of the clearest cases on this list where "no good public transport link exists" is simply the accurate answer rather than an exaggeration.

## Naples Airport: gateway to Sorrento, the Amalfi Coast, Pompeii, and Capri

[Naples Airport](/airport-transfers/naples) (Capodichino) is compact and close to the city, but its real value for many travelers is as the entry point to the Sorrentine Peninsula and the Amalfi Coast — destinations that are notoriously awkward to reach directly from Rome and much more naturally approached from here.

### Onward to Sorrento

Sorrento is about 50 km from the airport. Driving takes roughly 54 minutes to just over an hour outside peak traffic. There is a direct bus from the airport straight to Sorrento's train station, taking around 75 minutes — a genuinely useful option if it fits your arrival time. The alternative public route, via central Naples with a transfer, stretches to nearly 3 hours, which is a poor trade against the direct bus or a private transfer covering the same ground in under an hour and a half.

### Onward to the Amalfi Coast

Positano, as a reference point on the [Amalfi Coast](/destinations/amalfi-coast), sits about 61 km from the airport. In light traffic that's just over an hour; in the congestion typical of summer weekends, it can stretch to 2 to 2.5 hours. There's no direct bus or train to the Amalfi towns from the airport — the standard public route goes through Naples or Sorrento first, then picks up a SITA coastal bus, which is slow, frequently full in high season, and involves standing with luggage on switchback roads. This is the single clearest case on this whole list for booking a direct private transfer: the road distance is short, but the fragmented public transport option adds hours and discomfort for very little savings.

### Onward to Pompeii and Herculaneum

Pompeii is close — about 24–28 km, with driving times in the 25–40 minute range depending on airport traffic. A direct bus runs from the airport to Pompeii five times a day in about 20 minutes, which is hard to beat if the schedule matches your arrival. Otherwise, both archaeological sites sit directly on the Circumvesuviana suburban rail line from Naples, making them an easy add-on for anyone already routing through the city center.

### Onward to Capri (via ferry)

Capri requires a ferry, and that ferry departs from Naples' port, not the airport. Getting from Capodichino to the port — Molo Beverello or Calata Porta di Massa — takes about 25–30 minutes by taxi or the Alibus shuttle. From there, hydrofoils reach Capri in as little as 50 minutes to just over an hour. Because this route already involves a mandatory mode change (car or bus, then boat), a private transfer's main advantage is timing the port arrival precisely against ferry departures rather than budgeting a wide margin for a public bus that may run behind schedule.

## Florence Airport: gateway to the Tuscan countryside

Florence Airport (Peretola, also called Amerigo Vespucci) sits just a few kilometers from the city center, but its onward role is really about the Tuscan hinterland — Chianti, Siena, and the smaller hill towns that don't have their own airport.

### Onward to Siena

[Siena](/destinations/siena) is about 98 km away by road, and driving covers it in roughly an hour. Rail is the weaker option here: there's no direct train from the airport, so the trip means reaching Florence's main station first, then a regional or bus connection to Siena — a combined journey of around 2.5 hours once transfers are counted. For a destination that's only an hour away by car, that gap is wide enough that most travelers without a rail pass already in hand find a direct transfer or a rental car more sensible.

### Onward to Chianti

The [Chianti](/destinations/tuscany) wine region doesn't have a single arrival point — driving times from the airport range from about 40 minutes to the nearer edge of the region up to 90 minutes for towns further south like Radda or Gaiole. There's no meaningful public transport network connecting scattered wineries and agriturismi, which makes this one of the more clear-cut car-dependent regions on this list: a rental car or a private driver are the only realistic ways to reach most Chianti accommodations directly, since the appeal of the region is precisely in the small, spread-out estates that no train line serves.

## Pisa Airport: gateway to Florence, Tuscany, and the Cinque Terre

Pisa Airport is smaller than Florence's but handles significant low-cost carrier traffic, which makes it a common — if slightly overlooked — entry point for travelers heading to [Pisa](/destinations/pisa) itself and well beyond the Leaning Tower.

### Onward to Florence

Florence is about 80 km from Pisa Airport by road, roughly an hour's drive. By train, the PisaMover shuttle connects the airport to Pisa Centrale in about 5 minutes, and from there frequent Trenitalia services reach Florence in around 1 hour 20 minutes, for a total journey of about 1.5 hours including the connection. This is one of the more evenly matched routes on this list — train and car cover it in similar time, so the choice comes down to luggage and preference rather than one option being clearly faster.

### Onward to the Cinque Terre

The Cinque Terre villages sit about 86 km up the coast from Pisa. Driving takes roughly 1 hour 30 minutes, though it's worth noting that cars aren't allowed into the villages themselves, so a drive ends at a parking area outside the towns. By train, the PisaMover connection to Pisa Centrale feeds into direct Intercity services reaching Monterosso in about 1 hour 10 minutes — genuinely one of the faster and more convenient rail connections on this entire guide, and arguably the better choice over driving specifically because the Cinque Terre's own local trains, not cars, are how you move between the five villages once you arrive.

### Onward to wider Tuscany

Beyond Florence, Pisa also functions as a low-cost gateway into western Tuscany more broadly — Lucca, the Tuscan coast, and the Tuscan countryside toward Siena, sometimes covered as a [Florence to Pisa](/routes/florence-to-pisa) routing in reverse. As with Chianti from Florence Airport, the more rural and scattered the destination, the more a rental car or private transfer outperforms rail, which is built around fixed stations rather than countryside estates.

| Rome Ciampino | Central Rome mainly; longer legs route via Fiumicino-style options | Train/bus combination is limited; private transfer most practical for onward legs |
| Milan Malpensa | Lake Como, Lake Maggiore, northern Italy | Direct bus to Stresa; train with change via Saronno to Como; car or transfer for lakeside villages |

## Choosing between the three real options

Across every route above, the choice comes down to the same three options, and the right one depends on the same few variables each time.

- **Direct private transfer**: best when the public transport route is indirect, involves multiple changes, or runs on a schedule that doesn't match your flight — which describes most of the longer connections in this guide, from Rome to the Amalfi Coast to Venice's airport into the Dolomites. Fixed price, no changeovers, and flight-tracked pickup remove the variables that make these longer legs stressful with luggage.
- **Train, with or without a change**: strongest when a genuine direct or near-direct rail link exists — Pisa to the Cinque Terre, Venice's airport (via Mestre) to Padua, Rome to Naples once you've reached Termini. Weakest wherever a route requires reaching a main station first and then a second regional service, which erodes the time and cost advantage rail normally has.
- **Rental car**: the right call for destinations that are themselves spread out and don't cluster around a station — Chianti, the wider Tuscan countryside, multi-village lake touring on Como or Maggiore. Less appealing for the Amalfi Coast specifically, where narrow, congested roads make self-driving a genuine source of stress rather than convenience for first-time visitors.

None of these onward journeys are difficult once you know which category they fall into. The distances involved — mostly 50 to 280 km — put everything in this guide within a single day's travel from any of Italy's major airports; the only real decision is which mode matches the specific gap between where your plane lands and where you're actually trying to go.
