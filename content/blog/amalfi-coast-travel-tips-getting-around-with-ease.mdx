There isn't one right way to get around the Amalfi Coast, and that's actually the useful thing to know before you go. Walking, the SITA bus, the seasonal ferries, and a private driver all have a real place on this coast, and the trip goes more smoothly when you match the right one to the right leg of the journey rather than defaulting to a single method for everything.

**Getting around the Amalfi Coast** comes down to a few honest trade-offs: cost against convenience, schedule against flexibility, and how much of your limited vacation time you're willing to spend managing logistics instead of just being on the coast. Here's how those options actually compare, leg by leg.

## Walking: Right Inside the Towns, Not Between Them

Once you're inside Positano, Amalfi, or Ravello, walking isn't just an option — for large stretches of each town, it's the only option. These towns were built long before cars, on terrain that in many places still doesn't accommodate them, so exploring the historic lanes, the stepped alleys, and the viewpoints is inherently on foot regardless of how you arrived.

Where walking stops making sense is between towns. The coastal road connecting Positano, Amalfi, and Ravello is not a pleasant or safe walk — it's a narrow, winding road built for vehicles, with limited or no pedestrian space along most of its length. Nobody is realistically walking from Positano to Amalfi along the coast road itself. That leg needs one of the other three options.

## The SITA Bus: Cheap, Frequent, and Genuinely Crowded in Season

The SITA bus is the backbone of budget travel along this coast, running between the main towns with reasonable frequency and at a fraction of the cost of a private transfer. For a traveler without much luggage, some flexibility in timing, and a tolerance for standing in a crowd, it's a perfectly workable way to get between towns.

The honest trade-off is capacity. In peak season, buses on the main coastal route regularly run full, especially at popular departure times, and there's no guarantee of a seat or even of getting on the first bus that arrives. The road itself adds its own texture to the experience — the same switchbacks that make the coast scenic make a full, warm bus a genuinely uncomfortable ride for some travelers, particularly anyone prone to motion sickness. It's also not a practical option with a full set of luggage or a stroller, since space is limited and priority isn't guaranteed.

## Ferries: Scenic, Seasonal, and Limited to Certain Routes

Where they run, ferries connecting coastal towns are one of the more pleasant ways to travel — the ride offers views of the coastline you don't get from the road, and it skips the winding traffic entirely. The catch is that ferry service is seasonal, generally running in the warmer months and scaling back or stopping in winter, and it only connects certain towns directly by water rather than offering a complete network between every stop on the coast. Weather can also affect service on a given day. Worth checking current schedules before building a day around one, since routes and frequency change year to year.

## Private Transportation: The Option for Timing and Comfort

A private driver doesn't compete with walking, the bus, or the ferry so much as it solves a different problem: control over timing, comfort on a genuinely winding road, and not having to manage the connections between all of the above yourself. This matters most in a few specific situations.

**Airport arrivals and departures** are the clearest case. Coming off a flight into Naples International Airport with luggage, or heading back for a departure, is exactly the kind of transfer where a pre-arranged pickup — someone waiting with your name, a vehicle already sized for your group and bags — removes a layer of stress that a bus connection or taxi search would add on top of an already long travel day.

**Day trips with a fixed itinerary**, like visiting two or three towns in a single day, benefit from a driver who waits or returns on your schedule rather than a shared timetable. If you want two hours in Ravello and forty-five minutes in Amalfi rather than whatever a bus schedule allows, a private vehicle is what makes that actually possible.

**Groups and families** generally do better with a private vehicle than with public transport, simply because keeping a group of five or more together on a crowded bus, or coordinating multiple independent taxis, tends to create more friction than it saves in cost. For a look at how vehicle sizing works for larger parties, see our guide to [private transportation for families and groups](/blog/amalfi-coast-private-transportation-for-families-and-groups).

**Anyone prone to motion sickness or uneasy on cliffside roads** may simply find a private vehicle more comfortable than a full bus taking the same switchbacks at the same pace, with more room and the ability to ask for a stop if needed.

## Renting a Car: Worth Thinking Through Before You Commit

Some visitors consider renting a car for the trip, and it's worth being clear-eyed about what that actually involves on this particular coast. The road connecting Positano, Amalfi, and Ravello is narrow and winding, with tight curves, occasional two-way sections that feel closer to one lane, and heavy traffic in peak season that can turn a short distance into a long, tense drive for anyone unfamiliar with the route. Parking adds its own difficulty once you arrive: the towns themselves have limited parking, often some distance from the historic centers, and what's available tends to be expensive relative to what you'd pay elsewhere. Several town centers also restrict vehicle access for non-residents entirely, so a rental car frequently ends up parked outside the center you actually came to see, with a walk still required to get in.

None of this makes self-driving impossible, and plenty of visitors do it. It just means a rental car doesn't remove the logistics of this coast so much as shift them — from managing a bus schedule to managing a narrow road and a parking search. A private driver who already knows the route and where a given hotel or drop-off point actually is removes both problems at once, which is a meaningful part of why so many visitors choose not to drive themselves here.

## Mixing Methods Across a Single Trip

None of this has to be an all-or-nothing choice. A common and sensible approach is to use a private transfer for the legs that carry the most logistics — the airport run, arrival and departure days, a specific day trip with a tight schedule — and to walk or use the bus for the more flexible, lower-stakes parts of a trip, like an afternoon spent wandering Amalfi's center with no fixed plan. Matching the method to what that particular leg of the trip actually needs, rather than picking one approach for the whole visit, tends to produce the smoothest overall experience.

## A Few Practical Notes

- Check SITA bus and ferry schedules close to your travel dates, since frequency shifts by season
- If you're traveling with a stroller, significant luggage, or a group larger than three or four, a private vehicle is usually the more practical choice over public transport
- Book a private transfer for your airport arrival and departure regardless of how you plan to get around once you're on the coast, since that leg carries the most risk of a difficult, unpredictable connection
- Keep a flexible mindset around ferry schedules specifically, since weather can affect service on a given day

## Putting a Plan Together

Getting around the Amalfi Coast well is less about choosing one perfect method and more about being honest with yourself about what each leg of your trip actually needs — a scenic, unhurried walk through a town center, a budget-friendly bus ride with some flexibility, a seasonal ferry for the view, or a private driver when timing, comfort, or luggage make that the better call.
