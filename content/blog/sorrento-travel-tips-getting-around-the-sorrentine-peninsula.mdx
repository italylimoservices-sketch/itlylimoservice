Getting around Sorrento itself and getting around the wider Sorrentine Peninsula are two different problems, and conflating them is where a lot of trip planning goes wrong. Sorrento town is compact and walkable. The peninsula around it — the coastal towns, the hill villages, the routes toward Naples, Pompeii, and the Amalfi Coast — is a different matter entirely, involving narrow roads, limited public transit options, and drive times that shift with the season. This guide is a general orientation to both halves of that picture.

## Sorrento Town: Mostly a Walking Destination

Within Sorrento itself, walking is usually the right call. The historic center around Piazza Tasso, Corso Italia, and the streets leading down toward Marina Grande and Marina Piccola are close together, largely flat compared to the Amalfi Coast towns, and easy to cover without a vehicle. Many of the historic center's streets also have limited vehicle access, so a car often can't get you meaningfully closer to your destination than your own feet can once you're already in town.

A few practical notes for walking Sorrento:

- Comfortable shoes matter more than they might seem to — the streets are largely paved and level, but stone surfaces get slippery when wet
- The main sights, shops, and restaurants cluster closely enough that a full day of walking rarely means covering more than a few kilometers total
- Marina Grande and Marina Piccola both involve a walk down from the main town level, so factor that into plans if mobility is a consideration

For getting around inside Sorrento, a private vehicle mostly isn't the tool for the job — it's what happens beyond the town that changes the picture.

## Local Buses and Ferries

Local buses (notably the SITA line) connect Sorrento to towns along the Amalfi Coast, and ferries run seasonally between Sorrento, Capri, Naples, and Amalfi Coast towns depending on the time of year. Both can work well for a solo traveler or a couple with a flexible schedule and light luggage. They come with real trade-offs, though: buses on the coastal road can be standing-room-only during peak season, schedules are fixed rather than built around your day, and ferry timetables shift seasonally and can be affected by weather. Neither option is well suited to a family with young children, a group with real luggage, or anyone who wants to set their own pace for a day trip.

## When a Private Driver Is Worth It on the Peninsula

A private chauffeur earns its place on the parts of a Sorrento-based trip where flexibility and reliability matter more than saving money on the way there. A few scenarios where that's clearly the case:

**Arrival and departure.** Getting to Sorrento from Naples Airport, or heading back for a flight, is worth arranging in advance regardless of how the rest of your trip is planned. There's no dependable fixed duration to quote for this route, since traffic and time of day both affect it meaningfully, but a pre-arranged pickup means someone is waiting with your name at arrivals rather than you figuring out a taxi line after a long flight. Our guide to [arrival planning from Naples Airport](/blog/naples-airport-to-sorrento-arrival-planning-guide) covers this leg in more detail.

**Amalfi Coast day trips.** The road from Sorrento to Positano, Amalfi, and Ravello is narrow, scenic, and often slow, particularly in summer. A private vehicle means leaving when you're ready, stopping for photos or a break without losing a bus seat, and heading back on your own schedule rather than a fixed departure time. See our guide on [getting from Sorrento to the Amalfi Coast](/blog/sorrento-to-amalfi-coast-private-transfer-what-travelers-should-know) for more on that specific route.

**Pompeii and inland sights.** A day trip to Pompeii involves a different kind of travel than the coastal drives — more direct, but still worth arranging without depending on train connections, especially if your day includes a lot of walking once you arrive at the ruins themselves.

**Groups and families with luggage.** Anyone traveling with young children, older relatives, or more bags than a public bus comfortably accommodates will generally find a private vehicle less stressful, even for a relatively short trip.

## When Public Transit or Walking Makes More Sense

It's worth being honest that a private chauffeur isn't the answer to everything. Within Sorrento's own center, it adds little value over walking. A solo traveler with light luggage and a flexible schedule, visiting Capri for a day, may find the ferry perfectly workable and simpler than arranging a car-and-boat combination. The right approach usually depends on group size, luggage, schedule flexibility, and how much the day's plan depends on timing working out precisely.

## Orienting Yourself to the Peninsula's Geography

The Sorrentine Peninsula sits between the Bay of Naples and the start of the Amalfi Coast, with Sorrento itself on the northern, Naples-facing side. This geography matters for planning: the drive to Naples and its airport heads north and west, while the drive to the Amalfi Coast towns heads south and east along a coastal road that's a different driving experience entirely — narrower, more winding, and with sea views that come at the cost of speed. Knowing which direction a given day trip is heading helps set realistic expectations for how the drive itself will feel, even without a precise time estimate attached to it.

Inland from the coast, the peninsula also has quieter hill towns and agricultural areas less visited by typical itineraries. These aren't usually a first-time visitor's priority, but they're worth knowing about if your trip has more than the standard few days built in and you're looking for something beyond the main coastal towns.

## Timing Your Days Around the Peninsula's Traffic Patterns

Traffic on the Sorrento–Amalfi Coast road builds through the morning and can be at its worst around midday in peak season, easing somewhat by late afternoon before building again as day-trippers head back toward Sorrento and Naples in the evening. There's no way to promise a specific window will be clear, since it varies by day and season, but an earlier start to a day trip is generally a reasonable hedge against the busiest stretch of the day. This is worth factoring into planning even when the exact drive time can't be pinned down in advance.

## Putting a Plan Together

A sensible way to think about getting around the Sorrentine Peninsula: walk Sorrento itself, since that's where it's genuinely easy to do so; use a private vehicle for the arrival, the departure, and any day trip along the coast where flexibility and comfort matter more than cost; and consider public buses or seasonal ferries only if your schedule is flexible, your luggage is light, and standing for part of the trip isn't a dealbreaker. None of this is a strict rule — it's simply where each option tends to make the most sense based on how the peninsula is actually laid out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a car or driver to get around Sorrento town itself?**
No — Sorrento's historic center is compact and largely walkable, and many of its streets have limited vehicle access in any case. A private driver is more useful for trips beyond the town.

**Is the SITA bus a good option for day trips along the Amalfi Coast?**
It can work for a traveler with a flexible schedule and light luggage, but it's often standing-room-only during peak season and runs on a fixed timetable rather than your own schedule.

**How long does it take to drive from Sorrento to the Amalfi Coast?**
There's no dependable fixed duration to quote, since traffic, season, and the specific destination town all affect it. It's sensible to plan the day around flexibility rather than a tight schedule.

**When is a private chauffeur worth arranging on the Sorrentine Peninsula?**
It's most useful for airport arrivals and departures, day trips along the Amalfi Coast road, Pompeii excursions, and any trip involving families, groups, or more luggage than public transit comfortably handles.
