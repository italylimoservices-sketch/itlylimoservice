Packing for a Tuscany trip is usually the easy part. The harder part, and the one travelers tend to underestimate, is what happens to that luggage once you're actually moving around the region — dragging a rolling suitcase over cobblestones, navigating a hill town where the car can't get within a few hundred meters of your hotel, or trying to fit a case of wine you bought that morning into a trunk that was already full.

Tuscany's charm is tied up in the same features that make luggage logistics genuinely inconvenient: old stone towns, narrow lanes, and a touring style that often means multiple stops in a single day rather than one hotel-to-hotel transfer. Planning around that, rather than discovering it mid-trip, makes a real difference.

## Cobblestones Are Harder on Luggage Than They Look

Most of Tuscany's hill towns and historic centers were paved centuries before anyone imagined a wheeled suitcase. The stones are uneven, often sloped, and can be slippery when wet. A soft-sided bag with sturdy handles tends to fare better here than a hard-shell case with small, delicate wheels, which can struggle on rough surfaces and get caught in gaps between stones. If your trip includes towns like Volterra, Montepulciano, or San Gimignano, it's worth assuming you'll be carrying or wheeling luggage over uneven ground at some point, and packing — or at least choosing your bags — with that in mind.

This matters more for a multi-stop touring day than for a simple hotel transfer, since a single hard pull from a car to a hotel lobby is a very different task than wheeling a bag through a town center to reach accommodation tucked into a pedestrian-only street.

## Vehicle Access to Historic Centers Is Genuinely Limited

Many of Tuscany's hill towns restrict vehicle access into their historic cores, sometimes entirely and sometimes through a permit system aimed at residents rather than visitors. This isn't a minor inconvenience to plan around — it's a structural feature of how these towns work, and it means a private transfer may need to drop you at the edge of a historic center rather than directly outside your hotel door, with the final stretch covered on foot.

Knowing this ahead of time changes how you pack. If your accommodation sits inside a limited-access zone, a smaller, more manageable bag for the final approach — even if it means consolidating from a larger suitcase before you arrive — can save a genuinely frustrating walk with an overloaded case in tow. A good driver familiar with a particular town will usually know the closest practical drop-off point, but that point still might not be your hotel's front door.

## Multi-Stop Days Change What "Enough Space" Means

A day that includes more than one town, a vineyard visit, and maybe a stop at a local market isn't just longer than a single transfer — it accumulates things. Wine bought along the way, olive oil, ceramics from a hill-town shop, and whatever else catches your eye over the course of the day all need somewhere to go, and that somewhere is usually the same vehicle that's already carrying your original luggage.

This is worth thinking through before the day starts rather than midway through it. If you know wine purchases are likely — a winery visit is often built into a Tuscany countryside day — mentioning that when you book gives the operator a chance to match you with a vehicle that has the trunk space to handle both your travel luggage and whatever you bring back with you. A case or two of wine takes up more room than people expect, and it doesn't pack down the way clothing does.

## Choosing the Right Vehicle for Your Luggage

Vehicle choice in Tuscany should account for luggage volume, not just passenger count, and the two don't always scale together. A couple on a wine-focused day trip might need more trunk space than a family of four on a straightforward airport transfer. Some general guidance:

- An [executive sedan](/fleet/executive-sedan) or [luxury sedan](/fleet/luxury-sedan) suits one or two travelers with standard luggage and no major additional purchases expected along the way.
- A [luxury SUV](/fleet/luxury-suv) fits up to five passengers and four suitcases, which usually leaves reasonable room for a moderate amount of wine or shopping on top of standard luggage.
- An [executive van](/fleet/executive-van) or [luxury van](/fleet/luxury-van) handles up to seven passengers and six suitcases, and is the more comfortable choice for a larger group or a day where significant wine purchases are part of the plan.

When requesting a quote, giving an honest account of both your luggage and anything you expect to pick up along the way helps ensure the vehicle suggested actually has room for the day you're planning, rather than a tight fit that becomes a problem by the third stop.

## Packing Strategy for a Multi-Town Tuscany Trip

A few habits tend to make luggage less of a headache across a Tuscany itinerary:

- **Pack one bag that can be carried, not just wheeled**, for hill-town stops where cobblestones and limited vehicle access make wheeling impractical.
- **Leave deliberate space in your main luggage**, or plan for a separate bag, if wine or other purchases are a likely part of your trip.
- **Keep valuables and anything fragile in a bag that stays with you** rather than checked into a trunk you won't access again until the next stop, particularly on days with several separate drop-offs.
- **Confirm your accommodation's access situation in advance** if it sits inside a historic center, so you know what the final approach on foot will actually involve.

## Airport Arrivals Set the Tone for the Rest of the Trip

If your Tuscany trip starts with an international flight, the first transfer matters more than it might seem. Arriving after a long journey with a full set of luggage and heading straight into a multi-stop itinerary is a lot to manage on the first day, and it's worth building in a lighter plan for that initial transfer rather than scheduling a packed touring day immediately off the plane. A private pickup means your luggage goes straight into a vehicle sized for it, without the added step of managing bags through a taxi line or a public transit connection while jet-lagged.

This is also the point where mismatched expectations about vehicle size tend to surface. Travelers sometimes underestimate how much space a full set of luggage takes up for a group of three or four, especially when everyone has a carry-on in addition to checked bags. Giving an accurate count at the time of booking — rather than rounding down — avoids arriving to find a vehicle that technically fits the passenger count but not the luggage that came with them.

## Returning Home With More Than You Arrived With

It's worth thinking ahead to the return leg of the trip as well. Travelers who've spent a week or two touring Tuscany's wineries and hill towns often go home with considerably more than they packed — bottles of wine, olive oil, ceramics, leather goods, and other souvenirs that didn't exist in the suitcase on the way in. If your departure transfer is booked well before you know exactly how much you'll be bringing home, it's worth erring toward a larger vehicle than you think you'll need, or flagging the likely volume when you confirm your final transfer details closer to the departure date.

## Why This Is Worth Planning Around, Not Improvising

None of this is complicated once you know it, but it's easy to miss if your only frame of reference is city travel, where a car can usually get close to wherever you're staying. Tuscany's hill towns don't work that way, and a touring day that adds wine and shopping to the mix compounds the issue further. Flagging your luggage situation and your likely purchases when you book — rather than working it out on the day — is the simplest way to avoid an uncomfortable surprise partway through a trip.
