## 1. Decide when to go

Season shapes almost everything else about an Italy trip: prices, crowd sizes, what the weather will actually feel like, and even which regions make sense. June through August is peak season — long days and a lively atmosphere, but also the highest prices, the thickest crowds at major sights, and the hardest hotel and train availability. April–May and September–October are generally the sweet spot, with comfortable weather and noticeably thinner crowds. Winter brings the lowest prices and the quietest sights outside a handful of holiday weeks, though some coastal and rural attractions scale back their hours or close entirely.

The right month also depends on what you want from the trip and where you're going — a beach-and-coast itinerary in Puglia or the Amalfi Coast has a much narrower ideal window than a city-focused one built around museums and food, and the south stays warm and workable well past when the north cools off. If your dates are fixed by work or school schedules rather than open, the month-by-month guide is the faster read: it tells you what to actually expect in your specific travel window instead of general seasonal advice.

## 2. Decide how long to stay and where to go

Once you have a season, settle two more things: how many days you have, and how many places you'll actually try to see in them. A common mistake is picking a duration first and then cramming in too many cities — five nights split across four bases means more time on trains and in hotel lobbies than in front of the things you came to see.

As a rough anchor, a week is enough for one region done properly (Rome alone, or Florence plus a couple of Tuscan day trips); ten days lets you comfortably link two or three cities; two weeks or more opens up a genuine multi-region route without feeling rushed. Where you base yourself matters as much as how many nights you book — a well-placed hotel in the right neighborhood can save more time than an extra day added to a badly sequenced itinerary, and choosing between a single base with day trips versus a multi-city hop affects everything from how much luggage you'll want to how you'll move between stops.

## 3. Handle the planning logistics: budget, packing, and your checklist

With dates, duration, and a rough route settled, the remaining planning work is mostly administrative — figuring out what the trip will cost, what to bring, and making sure nothing with a hard deadline (passport validity, ticket releases, hotel deposits) slips through the cracks.

Budgeting for Italy varies enormously by season, city, and travel style — a shoestring trip through smaller towns and a five-star Rome-Florence-Venice loop can differ by a factor of five or more, so it's worth pricing out your actual route rather than relying on a single national average. Big cost drivers worth flagging early include peak-season hotel rates in Rome, Florence, and Venice; whether you're renting a car or relying on trains and transfers; and how many paid attraction tickets your itinerary needs.

Packing well matters more in Italy than in a lot of destinations, thanks to cobblestone streets, church dress codes that require covered shoulders and knees, and the amount of walking most itineraries involve — comfortable, broken-in shoes do more for a trip than almost any other packing decision. Italy runs on the standard European 230V supply and two-pin plugs, so non-European travelers will want an adapter packed before departure rather than sourced at the airport. And because several planning steps have real deadlines — visa applications, timed-entry museum tickets, high-speed train fares that get more expensive closer to departure — a checklist organized by how far out you are from your trip date is genuinely useful rather than a formality.

## 4. Know the culture and stay safe

Italy is an easy country to visit but an easy one to get small things wrong in — cappuccino after 11 a.m., tipping the way you would at home, or wandering into a church in a tank top and shorts won't ruin your trip, but knowing the local norms in advance makes everything smoother and marks you as less of an obvious target for the handful of scams that concentrate around tourist areas. Shops and smaller businesses in many towns still close for a few hours midafternoon, and meal times run later than in much of the English-speaking world — dinner rarely gets going before 7:30 or 8 p.m. Sightseeing itself also runs on its own logistics — reservation systems, opening-hour quirks, and queue conventions vary by site and city, and knowing them ahead of time saves real hours over a trip.

Safety in Italy's major cities is overwhelmingly about petty theft and tourist-targeted scams rather than violent crime — pickpocketing on crowded transit, overcharging at unmarked taxis, and a short list of well-documented street cons near major sights and train stations. Knowing what to watch for and where it tends to happen takes most of the risk off the table without requiring any real change to how you travel.

## 5. Plan your airport arrival and departure

Your airport choice is one of the earliest decisions that ripples through the rest of the trip, because it effectively sets which region your trip starts and ends in. Italy has four major international gateways — Rome Fiumicino, Milan Malpensa, Venice Marco Polo, and Naples Capodichino — plus several smaller airports (Rome Ciampino, Milan Linate and Bergamo, Florence, Pisa, Bologna, and Sicily's Catania and Palermo) that matter for budget carriers or specific regions. For a multi-city trip, flying into one gateway and out of another is often more efficient than looping back to your arrival airport, and it's worth deciding early since it changes how your route reads from end to end.

Beyond picking the right airport, the trip has two airport-specific phases worth planning separately: what happens between touchdown and getting to your first hotel, and what happens in the hours before your flight home. Both have their own timing rules — how early to arrive for security and check-in, how immigration and customs actually work on arrival, and how to avoid the last-day scramble of trying to buy souvenirs and get to the gate at the same time.

## 6. Get around once you're there

Italy's transportation options are genuinely good, which is also why choosing among them takes a bit of thought. High-speed trains (Trenitalia's Frecce services and the competing Italo) connect the major cities quickly and comfortably — Rome to Florence, for example, runs well under an hour and a half — making rail the default choice for a multi-city itinerary built around Rome, Florence, Venice, and Milan. Driving opens up the countryside routes trains can't reach — Tuscany's hill towns, the Amalfi Coast, rural Umbria — but comes with city-center restrictions (ZTL zones) and parking headaches best avoided in historic centers. Within cities, public transit (metro, buses, trams) and taxis or licensed private drivers (NCC) cover the rest, and for city pairs that don't sit on a convenient rail line, a private transfer often beats a train-plus-connection combination on both time and comfort.

## How to use this guide

Treat this page as a table of contents, not a to-do list to finish in one sitting. A practical order is: lock in a season and rough trip length first, sketch a route and where you'll stay, then work through budget, packing, and your checklist in parallel with booking flights and hotels, and leave the airport and in-country transportation decisions for once your route is closer to final — they depend on exactly which cities you're connecting. Come back to the table near the top whenever you lose track of what's next; it's built to be revisited rather than read once and forgotten.

None of these decisions are independent — moving your travel dates shifts hotel prices and ticket availability, and adding a city to your route changes your airport strategy and your intercity transportation. That's the real reason to work through this in roughly the order laid out above rather than jumping straight to booking flights: each stage narrows the options for the next one, and planning them in sequence means you make fewer decisions twice.

If you'd rather hand off the logistics than coordinate every leg yourself — airport pickup, city-to-city transfers, and a driver for day trips — an [Italy private tour](/italy-private-tours) or a standing [chauffeur service](/chauffeur-service) can fold most of the transportation decisions above into a single booking. That's especially worth considering for multi-city routes, trips with an older or less mobile traveler in the group, or itineraries where minimizing time spent coordinating logistics matters more than the cost difference versus handling each leg independently — you still make the same decisions about where and when, but someone else handles the getting-there.
