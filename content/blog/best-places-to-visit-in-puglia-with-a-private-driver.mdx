Puglia doesn't look like the rest of Italy most visitors picture. There's no single dominant city to anchor a trip the way Rome or Florence might — instead, the region forms the heel of Italy's boot, a long stretch of coastline and countryside dotted with towns that each have a genuinely different character. Whitewashed hill towns, a baroque city, a port city, and a scatter of seaside villages are all within reach of each other, but "within reach" in Puglia still means real driving distances, not a short stroll between neighborhoods.

## A Region Built From Contrasts

What makes Puglia interesting to plan around is how little its towns resemble each other. Alberobello's conical stone houses look almost nothing like Lecce's ornate baroque facades, which in turn look nothing like the sun-bleached white walls of Ostuni or the cliffside drama of Polignano a Mare. A trip that tries to lump these together as "a day in Puglia" tends to undersell all of them. A trip that treats each one as its own stop, with a driver handling the stretches of countryside and coast road in between, tends to come away with a much better sense of what the region actually offers.

## Alberobello and the Trulli

Alberobello is probably the single most recognizable name in Puglia, and for good reason. The town is built around clusters of trulli — small, conical-roofed limestone structures that are unique to this part of Italy and unlike anything else on the peninsula. Walking through the Rione Monti district, where most of the trulli are concentrated, is a strange and genuinely memorable experience, the kind of thing photographs don't quite prepare you for.

Because the trulli district is dense and best explored on foot, a car's job here is mostly about arrival and departure — getting close to the historic center, finding a sensible place to park or be dropped, and letting the walking happen without a vehicle in the mix. It's a town that rewards a slower visit rather than a quick drive-by.

## Polignano a Mare: The Town on the Cliff

Polignano a Mare sits directly on the Adriatic coast, built into limestone cliffs that drop straight into the sea. Its old town is compact, with narrow streets that open unexpectedly onto views of the water far below, and it has become known in particular for the view from its main bridge, where the old center frames a small cove that fills with swimmers in warmer months.

It's a town that works well as a shorter stop — a few hours is usually enough to walk the old center, take in the coastal views, and get a feel for the place — which makes it a strong candidate for visitors with limited time. ## Monopoli: A Quieter Port Town

A short distance from Polignano, Monopoli offers a different, less visited version of the same coastline — a working port town with its own old center, a harbor still used by local fishing boats, and a slower pace than some of the more heavily visited stops nearby. It doesn't have a single landmark that draws crowds the way Alberobello's trulli or Polignano's cliffs do, and that's part of its appeal. Visitors who want a look at coastal Puglia without the busier atmosphere of its best-known towns often find Monopoli a worthwhile detour.

## Ostuni: The White City

Inland and uphill from the coast, Ostuni earns its nickname — the White City — honestly. Its historic center is a maze of whitewashed buildings climbing a hillside, visible from a considerable distance across the surrounding countryside of olive groves. Walking up through the old town, with its narrow stepped streets and occasional sudden views out over the plain below, is a different kind of experience than the coastal towns nearby, more about the town's layout and setting than any single sight.

Ostuni's position means it pairs naturally with the countryside around it rather than strictly with the coast, and it tends to work well as either a standalone stop or part of a longer day that also includes a coastal town.

## Lecce: Puglia's Baroque City

Further south, Lecce is often described as the baroque capital of southern Italy, and its historic center backs that up with an unusual density of ornately carved churches and palazzi, many built from a local golden-hued limestone that takes detail well and has weathered into a distinctive warm color over the centuries. It's a larger, more urban stop than the hill towns and fishing villages further north, with a different rhythm — more café culture, more shops, a genuine city feel inside a historic shell.

Lecce sits a meaningful distance from Alberobello and the coastal towns around Bari, which generally makes it its own destination rather than something tacked onto a day built around the trulli or Polignano. Visitors basing themselves near Bari should treat a Lecce visit as a deliberate choice rather than an easy add-on.

## Bari: The Region's Main Hub

Bari functions as Puglia's largest city and the practical arrival point for most visitors, whether by air or by sea. Its old town, Bari Vecchia, is a dense warren of narrow streets built close to the harbor, with its own churches, markets, and the kind of everyday street life that makes it worth more than a passing look even though it's primarily known as a gateway city. ## How a Driver Handles the Distances Between Towns

There's no verified, fixed driving time between any of these towns — conditions vary with season, traffic around Bari in particular, and the state of the smaller rural roads that connect the inland towns to the coast. What can be said honestly is that Puglia's towns are spread across real distances, closer together than Sicily's major hubs but still far enough apart that stringing together more than two or three in a single day usually means rushing all of them.

This is where a private driver earns their place. Self-driving in Puglia means dealing with unfamiliar road signage, parking that's often limited or awkward in historic centers never designed for cars, and the mental overhead of navigating between towns while also trying to enjoy them. A chauffeur absorbs that entirely, and because travel times genuinely fluctuate day to day, a driver with current, local knowledge of the roads is worth more than any fixed estimate.

## Grouping Puglia Into Realistic Day Trips

Rather than trying to see everything in one pass, it tends to work better to think of Puglia's highlights in loose pairings:

- Alberobello and Ostuni, both inland, connected by countryside roads through olive groves
- Polignano a Mare and Monopoli, both coastal and close to Bari
- Lecce on its own, given its distance from the others and the amount it rewards a slower visit

A shorter trip usually means picking one of these groupings and giving it proper time rather than skimming across all three.
