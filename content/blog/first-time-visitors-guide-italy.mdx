Planning your first trip to Italy can feel a little overwhelming. There's Rome, Florence, Venice, Milan, Lake Como, Tuscany, the Amalfi Coast—and that's before you even start thinking about food, trains, hotels, airports, and what to pack.

The good news? You don't need to see everything in one trip.

Italy is best enjoyed at a comfortable pace. Rather than rushing from one city to another, choose a few destinations that fit your interests and build your itinerary around them. A little planning before you leave can also save you time, money, and plenty of unnecessary stress once you arrive.

This **first-time visitor's guide to Italy** covers the practical things you should know before your trip, including when to visit, how long to stay, getting around, where to go, what to expect from Italian cities, dining customs, safety, money, packing, and transportation.

Whether you're planning a short city break or a longer Italian holiday, here's what you need to know before you go.

## 1. Decide What Kind of Italy Trip You Want

Before booking flights and hotels, decide what you actually want from your trip.

Italy can be very different depending on your priorities.

Are you interested in:

- Ancient history?
- Art and architecture?
- Food and wine?
- Beaches?
- Luxury travel?
- Shopping?
- Romantic getaways?
- Family travel?
- Outdoor adventures?
- Business?
- Road trips?

Your answer should influence where you go.

For example, someone interested in ancient history may prioritize Rome, while a food lover might spend more time in Bologna and Emilia-Romagna. A couple looking for a romantic escape might prefer Lake Como, Tuscany, Venice, or the Amalfi Coast.

Trying to squeeze every famous destination into one trip sounds exciting on paper, but it can quickly turn into a suitcase-and-train-station marathon.

## 2. Don't Try to See All of Italy in One Trip

Italy looks manageable on a map, but travel times add up.

Rome, Florence, Venice, Milan, Naples, Lake Como, and the Amalfi Coast aren't all next door to one another.

For a first trip, it's usually better to focus on one geographic area or a small number of cities.

### A Simple 5-Day Italy Trip

A short trip could focus on:

**Rome → Florence**

or simply:

**Rome**

### A 7-Day Italy Trip

You could consider:

**Rome → Florence → Venice**

or:

**Milan → Lake Como → Florence**

### A 10-Day Italy Trip

With more time, you could create a broader itinerary such as:

**Rome → Florence → Venice → Milan**

Another option is:

**Rome → Naples → Amalfi Coast → Florence**

The best itinerary depends on your interests and how much time you want to spend moving between destinations.

## 3. Choose the Best Time to Visit Italy

Italy is a year-round destination, but different seasons offer very different experiences.

### Spring

Spring can be a fantastic time to visit.

You'll generally find pleasant conditions for sightseeing, particularly in cities where you'll spend a lot of time walking.

Popular spring destinations include:

- Rome
- Florence
- Tuscany
- Venice
- Lake Como
- Amalfi Coast

April and May are especially popular, so don't assume every destination will be quiet.

### Summer

Summer is one of Italy's busiest travel periods.

June, July, and August can bring:

- Larger crowds
- Higher hotel prices
- Hot weather
- Busy attractions
- Heavy traffic in popular destinations

Coastal destinations can be especially popular during summer.

If you're visiting during peak season, book important hotels, attractions, and transportation ahead of time.

### Autumn

September and October can be excellent months for Italy.

Autumn is particularly appealing for:

- Tuscany
- Wine regions
- Florence
- Rome
- Northern Italy

The season also works well for travelers who want to combine sightseeing with food and wine experiences.

### Winter

Winter is often quieter in many parts of Italy.

Rome, Milan, Florence, and other major cities remain interesting outside the summer season.

Mountain destinations, meanwhile, become popular with travelers looking for winter sports.

## 4. How Many Days Do You Need in Italy?

There's no single perfect answer.

If it's your first visit, **7–10 days** gives you enough time to experience several highlights without spending your entire holiday in transit.

### 3–4 Days

Choose one major city.

**Rome** is a strong choice for first-time visitors because it combines history, architecture, food, museums, and famous landmarks.

### 5–7 Days

Choose two or three destinations.

For example:

**Rome + Florence**

or:

**Milan + Lake Como**

### 8–10 Days

You can comfortably create a multi-city itinerary.

For example:

**Rome → Florence → Venice → Milan**

The key is not the number of cities you visit. It's how much time you actually have to enjoy them.

## 6. Know How Transportation Works in Italy

Getting around Italy is generally straightforward, but the best option depends on your route.

Your main choices are:

- High-speed trains
- Regional trains
- Buses
- Taxis
- Rental cars
- Private chauffeur services

### Trains

For major city-to-city journeys, trains can be extremely convenient.

High-speed rail connects several major cities, making journeys such as Rome to Florence or Florence to Milan practical for many travelers.

### Rental Cars

A rental car can be useful for countryside travel, especially in areas such as Tuscany.

However, driving in historic city centers can be challenging.

This brings us to one of Italy's most important driving rules: ZTL zones.

## 7. Understand Italy's ZTL Zones

**ZTL** stands for *Zona a Traffico Limitato*, meaning a restricted traffic zone.

These areas are common in historic city centers.

Certain vehicles aren't allowed to enter during restricted periods, and cameras may record license plates.

This matters because a visitor can accidentally drive into a restricted zone and receive a traffic fine later.

ZTL areas can be found in or around historic centers in cities including:

- [Florence](/destinations/florence)
- [Rome](/destinations/rome)
- [Milan](/destinations/milan)
- [Bologna](/destinations/bologna)
- [Pisa](/destinations/pisa)
- [Siena](/destinations/siena)
- [Verona](/destinations/verona)
- [Naples](/destinations/naples)

If you're renting a car, check the rules carefully before driving into a city center.

For travelers who don't want to deal with restricted zones, parking, unfamiliar roads, or navigation, public transportation or pre-arranged private transportation can be a simpler alternative.

For airport transfers, long-distance journeys, and multi-stop itineraries, a professional driver can also be useful when convenience is more important than driving yourself.

There's no need to choose one transportation method for your entire trip.

Use each option where it makes sense.

| Rome → Florence                         | High-speed train or private transfer                  |
| Florence → Venice                       | High-speed train or private transfer                  |
| Milan → Lake Como                       | Train, car or private transportation                  |
| Tuscany countryside                     | Car or private driver                                  |
| Amalfi Coast                            | Private transportation, taxi or other local options    |

A mixed approach often works best. If you'd rather not manage train connections and luggage changes yourself, a private [Rome to Florence transfer](/routes/rome-to-florence) can take you directly, door to door, on your own schedule.

## 9. Book Important Things in Advance

Italy's most popular attractions can get busy.

For a first-time trip, consider booking important experiences ahead of time, particularly during peak travel periods.

This can include:

- Popular museums
- Major attractions
- Guided tours
- High-speed trains
- Hotels
- Airport transportation
- Special restaurants
- Private tours

You don't have to schedule every minute of your holiday.

Leave room for spontaneous discoveries. Some of Italy's best moments happen when you turn down a quiet street, stop for coffee, or find a small restaurant that wasn't on your original itinerary.

## 10. Pack for Walking

Here's something first-time visitors sometimes underestimate:

**You'll probably walk a lot.**

Italian cities are best explored on foot, and historic centers often have cobblestone streets.

Pack:

- Comfortable walking shoes
- Lightweight clothing
- A small day bag
- Sunglasses
- Sunscreen
- Portable charger
- Reusable water bottle
- Light jacket
- Weather-appropriate layers

Avoid packing five pairs of shoes just because they look good with different outfits. Your feet will thank you later.

## 11. Dress Appropriately for Churches

Italy has countless beautiful churches and religious sites.

Some require visitors to dress modestly.

Depending on the location, you may need to cover:

- Shoulders
- Upper legs

If visiting major religious attractions, carrying a lightweight scarf or cover-up can be useful.

Comfortable but respectful clothing is usually the safest choice.

## 13. Understand Italian Meal Times

Dining habits can be different from what you're used to.

Lunch and dinner may happen later than in some countries, and meals can be more leisurely.

Traditional Italian meals can include several courses:

**Antipasto → Primo → Secondo → Contorno → Dolce**

You don't have to order every course.

If you're not very hungry, ordering a main dish and perhaps a side is perfectly reasonable.

## 14. Don't Expect Every Restaurant to Serve the Same Food

Italy doesn't have one single cuisine.

Food varies dramatically from region to region.

In Rome, you may encounter famous pasta dishes such as carbonara and cacio e pepe.

In Bologna and Emilia-Romagna, you'll find specialties associated with the region's rich culinary traditions.

In Naples, pizza is a major part of the food culture.

Along the coast, seafood features heavily.

In Tuscany, you'll find hearty regional dishes and excellent wine.

So don't just look for "Italian food."

Look for **regional Italian food**.

## 15. Understand Coffee Culture

Coffee is serious business in Italy.

You'll see locals stopping briefly for an espresso at a café, often drinking it at the counter.

If you order a "latte," remember that *latte* means milk in Italian.

If you want a coffee with milk, specify the drink you actually want, such as a caffè latte or cappuccino.

And don't be surprised if your cappuccino comes with a smaller portion than the giant coffee-shop drinks you may be used to.

## 16. Know How Tipping Works

Tipping in Italy isn't always handled the same way as in countries where large percentage-based tips are expected.

Service charges and restaurant practices can vary.

Check your bill before deciding whether an additional tip is appropriate.

For exceptional service, leaving something extra can be a nice gesture.

For drivers and private transportation providers, tipping practices can also vary, so you shouldn't assume that the same rules apply everywhere.

## 17. Carry Some Cash, But Don't Rely Only on Cash

Cards are widely accepted in many parts of Italy, especially in cities and tourist areas.

Still, carrying some cash can be useful for:

- Small purchases
- Markets
- Small cafés
- Tips
- Unexpected situations

Don't carry large amounts unnecessarily.

A combination of a payment card and a modest amount of cash is a sensible approach.

## 18. Keep Your Belongings Secure

Italy is generally a popular and rewarding destination, but busy tourist areas can attract pickpockets.

Be particularly careful in crowded places.

Keep an eye on your belongings around:

- Major train stations
- Crowded public transport
- Popular attractions
- Busy squares
- Tourist-heavy streets
- Large events

Practical precautions include:

- Keep your phone secure
- Use a zipped bag
- Don't leave valuables unattended
- Keep passports in a secure place
- Be cautious when someone creates an unusual distraction

You don't need to be paranoid.

Just stay aware.

## 19. Keep Your Passport Safe

Your passport is one of the most important things you carry.

Don't leave it casually in a restaurant, taxi, hotel lobby, or sightseeing bag.

Keep important documents secure and consider having copies or secure digital backups of key travel documents.

Before traveling, also make sure your passport validity meets the requirements that apply to your nationality and destination.

## 20. Check Entry Requirements Before You Travel

Entry requirements depend on your nationality, travel purpose, and circumstances.

Before departure, check current official requirements for:

- Passport validity
- Visa requirements
- Permitted length of stay
- Travel authorization requirements, where applicable
- Health or documentation requirements
- Customs rules

Don't rely on an old blog post for immigration information.

Rules can change, so check official government and consular sources shortly before your trip.

## 21. Think Carefully About Where You Stay

Location can make a huge difference.

A cheaper hotel far from the main attractions isn't necessarily cheaper if you spend hours and money commuting.

When choosing accommodation, consider:

- Distance from major attractions
- Nearby public transport
- Train station access
- Airport access
- Restaurants
- Walkability
- Neighborhood atmosphere
- Luggage access
- Late-night transportation

For short city breaks, staying centrally can save valuable time.

## 22. Don't Overpack Your Itinerary

This is probably one of the best Italy travel tips for first-time visitors.

You don't need to visit ten attractions every day.

Instead, choose a few priorities.

For example:

**Morning:** Major attraction
**Lunch:** Local restaurant
**Afternoon:** Neighborhood exploration
**Evening:** Dinner and relaxed sightseeing

That's much more enjoyable than racing across town from one attraction to another.

## 23. Plan Around Travel Time

When creating your itinerary, remember that "travel time" isn't just the time shown on Google Maps or a train schedule.

You may need time to:

- Leave the hotel
- Reach the station
- Find the platform
- Store luggage
- Wait for transportation
- Walk from the station
- Check into your hotel
- Deal with traffic

Build some breathing room into your schedule.

This is particularly important when connecting flights, trains, tours, or important business appointments.

## 24. Consider Private Transportation for Complex Journeys

Public transportation is excellent for many Italian routes.

But there are times when a private vehicle makes more sense.

Consider private transportation when you're:

- Traveling with a family
- Carrying lots of luggage
- Visiting several smaller towns
- Traveling through rural areas
- Planning a multi-stop itinerary
- Attending a wedding
- Traveling for business
- Going directly from an airport to a distant destination

For example, a traveler visiting [Tuscany](/destinations/tuscany) might prefer a private driver for a day involving several countryside stops rather than dealing with separate trains and taxis.

Likewise, families traveling from an airport directly to a hotel can benefit from having a vehicle arranged in advance.

## 27. Plan Airport Transportation Before Arrival

After a long flight, the last thing you want is to land and start figuring out transportation from scratch.

Before your trip, check:

- Which airport you're using
- How far it is from your hotel
- Public transportation options
- Taxi options
- Private transfer options
- Estimated travel time
- Your arrival terminal
- Your flight details

If you're traveling with several people or lots of luggage, compare vehicle capacity carefully before booking.

A private [airport transfer](/airport-transfers) can be particularly convenient for families, groups, and travelers arriving late.

## 28. Give Yourself Extra Time for Italian Traffic

Traffic varies significantly between cities and regions.

Urban traffic can become particularly heavy around airports, business districts, major attractions, and peak travel periods.

Don't schedule a flight immediately after a long road journey without allowing a sensible buffer.

The same applies to important meetings.

If you absolutely have to be somewhere at 9:00 AM, don't plan to arrive at 8:55.

Give yourself some breathing room.

## 29. Understand That Historic Centers Can Be Complicated

Many Italian cities have historic areas that weren't designed for modern traffic.

You may encounter:

- Narrow streets
- Pedestrian zones
- Limited vehicle access
- ZTL restrictions
- Limited parking
- Crowded roads

This is one reason transportation planning matters.

Sometimes the easiest option isn't the one that looks cheapest on paper.

## 30. Travel Insurance Is Worth Considering

Travel insurance can provide protection against certain unexpected situations, depending on the policy.

Possible areas of coverage can include:

- Trip cancellation
- Travel interruption
- Medical emergencies
- Lost luggage
- Delays
- Other covered incidents

Read the actual policy terms before purchasing.

Don't assume every situation is automatically covered.

## 31. Keep Emergency Information Handy

Save important numbers and information before you travel.

In Italy, **112** is the general European emergency number.

You should also know:

- Your accommodation address
- Your travel insurance information
- Your airline details
- Your embassy or consulate contact information
- Emergency contacts at home

Keep this information somewhere accessible even if your phone battery dies.

## 32. Respect Local Customs

A little courtesy goes a long way.

Simple things like:

- Greeting people politely
- Waiting your turn
- Speaking at a reasonable volume
- Dressing appropriately in religious places
- Respecting queues
- Following local rules

can make your experience smoother.

Italy is relaxed in many ways, but that doesn't mean every situation works exactly like home.

## 33. Don't Expect Everything to Happen on Your Schedule

Italy has a more relaxed rhythm in many situations.

Meals can take time.

Shops may have different opening hours.

Small businesses may operate differently from large chains.

Transportation can occasionally be affected by delays or disruptions.

The best approach?

Build some flexibility into your plans.

If your itinerary has zero room for anything unexpected, one small delay can throw the whole day off.

## Final Thoughts: Planning Your First Trip to Italy

Your first trip to Italy doesn't need to be perfect.

In fact, trying to make it perfect can take some of the fun out of it.

Choose a few destinations, understand how you'll travel between them, book the important things ahead of time, and leave some room for the unexpected.

Spend enough time in each place to actually experience it. Have that long lunch. Stop for coffee. Wander down the side street. Try a regional dish you can't pronounce. And don't worry if you miss one attraction from your list—you'll have a reason to come back.

From [Rome](/destinations/rome)'s ancient streets and [Florence](/destinations/florence)'s Renaissance treasures to [Milan](/destinations/milan)'s modern energy, [Lake Como](/destinations/lake-como)'s scenery, [Tuscany](/destinations/tuscany)'s countryside, and the [Amalfi Coast](/destinations/amalfi-coast)'s dramatic coastline, Italy offers something for almost every kind of traveler.

The key is choosing the right itinerary for **your** trip.

And when a journey involves several destinations, lots of luggage, children, business meetings, or complicated transfers, arranging transportation in advance can make a big difference.

For travelers who prefer a comfortable, door-to-door option, a [private chauffeur service in Italy](/chauffeur-service) can be useful for airport transfers, city-to-city journeys, business travel, and customized itineraries.
