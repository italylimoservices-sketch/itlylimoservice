Booking a private transfer from Naples to Positano is one thing. Knowing what the drive itself actually feels like is another, and it's the part most people don't think about until they're in the car. The route changes character completely partway through, and understanding that shift — where it happens, what it looks like, and how it might affect you — makes the two-plus hours (give or take, depending on the day) a lot more enjoyable than going in blind.

## The First Stretch: Leaving Naples Behind

However your trip starts — from a hotel in central Naples or from Naples Airport, which sits roughly 7 km and about 15 to 20 minutes from the city center — the opening part of the drive looks like driving almost anywhere in southern Italy. Wide roads, standard highway or dual-carriageway conditions, other traffic moving at normal speed. It's unremarkable in the best sense: smooth, fast, and nothing to brace for.

This part of the journey is worth using to settle in. Get comfortable, have water within reach, and if you or anyone traveling with you is prone to motion sickness, this is the stretch to take any precautions in — not after the road has already started winding. Once the character of the drive changes, it changes for a while.

## Where the Drive Actually Changes

There's a clear point on any trip toward the Amalfi Coast where the road stops behaving like a normal road and starts behaving like the Amalfi Coast. Exactly where that happens depends on the route your driver takes and conditions on the day, but broadly speaking, it's once you leave the faster roads around Naples and the Sorrentine Peninsula behind and join the coastal road that links the towns together.

From that point on, the road narrows. It's cut into the cliffside, tracing the contour of the coastline rather than cutting through it, which means it turns constantly — left, right, left again — as it follows the shape of the hills dropping into the sea. There's rarely a straight stretch of more than a few hundred meters. This is the part of the trip people mean when they talk about the Amalfi Coast's "switchback" driving: a continuous series of curves, many of them tight, with the road itself doing most of the visual work that a scenic overlook would do elsewhere.

## What It Actually Feels Like to Ride Through

Riding this stretch as a passenger is a different experience than reading about it. The turns come frequently enough that conversation tends to pause naturally — not because anything's wrong, but because there's usually a view worth looking at around the next bend. Terraced hillsides covered in lemon groves, small clusters of pastel houses stacked above the water, and long drops down to the sea are all part of the passing scenery, sometimes within the same few hundred meters.

Speeds on this stretch are naturally lower than on a highway, both because of the road's shape and because of local traffic — scooters, delivery vans, and other cars working the same narrow lanes. A private driver who knows the road well will manage pacing and spacing in a way that a self-driven rental car in unfamiliar hands generally can't, which is one of the more understated benefits of not driving it yourself: you can actually look at the coastline instead of watching the white line six feet in front of the bumper.

## Motion Sickness on a Road Like This

It's worth addressing directly, because the coast road's continuous curves genuinely do affect some passengers more than typical travel would. If you know you're prone to motion sickness, a few simple steps make a real difference: eat something light before the drive rather than nothing at all, avoid looking down at a phone or book for long stretches once the winding starts, and try to sit where you can see the road ahead through the windshield rather than facing sideways. Fresh air from a cracked window can help too. None of this guarantees comfort, but it meaningfully reduces the odds of the drive becoming unpleasant.

If you do start to feel unwell, it's completely reasonable to ask your driver to pull over briefly. There are places along the route where a car can stop safely, and a five-minute break is a small price for the rest of the drive going smoothly.

## Viewpoints and Photo Stops Along the Way

Part of what makes this drive memorable is that a lot of its best moments aren't at Positano itself — they're on the approach to it. Certain bends in the road open onto wide views of the coastline, with the town itself sometimes visible from a distance, tumbling down its hillside toward the water, well before you actually arrive.

Whether there's time to stop for photos depends on traffic, the day's schedule, and how much flexibility your itinerary has. It's a reasonable thing to mention to your driver at the start of the trip if photo stops matter to you — a driver who knows the road usually knows which pull-offs offer the best views without adding much time to the overall drive. Not every stretch has a safe place to stop, so this is worth treating as something to ask about rather than assume.

## How the Drive Differs Depending on Season

The coast road doesn't feel the same in July as it does in November. In peak summer, expect more traffic sharing the road — tour buses, other private cars, and a steady stream of scooters — which can slow the pace considerably, especially approaching the villages themselves. Outside peak season, the same road can feel almost sleepy by comparison, with long stretches where the only company is the view.

Neither version is better or worse, exactly, but they're different experiences. A quieter shoulder-season drive gives you more room to enjoy the scenery at a relaxed pace, while a summer drive is livelier but requires more patience, particularly in the final approach into Positano.

## What Changes Once You're Close to Positano

As the road nears Positano, the driving experience shifts one more time. Traffic tends to bunch up, the road narrows further in places, and the pace naturally slows as vehicles maneuver around each other near the village entrances. This is normal and not a sign that anything has gone wrong — it's simply what the final approach into one of the coast's busiest towns looks like, especially in the middle of the day.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is the drive from Naples to Positano scary or dangerous?**
It's narrow and winding with frequent tight turns, which can feel unfamiliar if you've never driven or ridden a coastal road like it before. With an experienced local driver managing the pace and positioning, most passengers find it manageable and, for many, genuinely enjoyable.

**Will I get carsick on the way to Positano?**
Some passengers do, given the road's continuous curves. Eating lightly beforehand, sitting facing forward, and getting fresh air if needed all help. It's worth mentioning any concerns to your driver in advance.

**Can I ask my driver to stop for photos on the way to Positano?**
It's a reasonable request, and drivers familiar with the route often know which spots offer good views with a safe place to pull over. It's best to mention this at the start of the trip so it can be factored into the schedule.

**Does the drive feel different depending on the time of year?**
Yes. Summer brings noticeably more traffic on the coast road, especially near village centers, while the same drive outside peak season tends to feel calmer and less crowded.
