Picking a number of days for an Italy trip is only half the job — the harder part is deciding what actually goes into those days. A week, ten days, and two weeks call for genuinely different routes, not the same itinerary with a city or two trimmed off the end. Below are three complete, day-by-day plans — one built specifically for 7 days, one for 10, and one for 14 — each tested against real train timetables and transfer times so the pacing holds up once you're actually on the ground. Use whichever matches your trip length as a starting point, then adjust with the guidance at the end of each section.

A note on how these are built: every itinerary assumes you're flying into and out of Italy (usually via Rome or Milan), and every travel day accounts for the actual time on the move — not just the headline train duration, but the reality of checking out, getting to the station, and settling into a new city on arrival. Where a private transfer makes more sense than a train (rural Tuscany, the Amalfi Coast, transfers with luggage or a group), that's noted too.

## The 7-Day Italy Itinerary: Rome, Florence & Venice

This is the classic first-timer's triangle, and it earns that reputation because the logistics work cleanly. All three cities sit on Italy's high-speed rail spine, so you're never losing half a day to transport, and each city delivers a genuinely different flavor of Italy — ancient and papal Rome, Renaissance Florence, and waterborne Venice.

### Day 1: Arrive in Rome

Land, get to your hotel, and treat the day as a soft landing. A short walk to the [Trevi Fountain](/destinations/rome) or the Pantheon area is enough for day one — don't schedule anything that requires being sharp. If your flight lands early enough, a low-key evening stroll through Trastevere is a good way to adjust before the heavier sightseeing starts tomorrow.

### Day 2: Rome — Ancient Rome

This is a full day around the Colosseum, Roman Forum, and Palatine Hill. Book timed-entry tickets in advance; this cluster alone easily fills four to five hours. Round out the afternoon at Piazza Navona or Campo de' Fiori.

### Day 3: Rome — Vatican City

The Vatican Museums and Sistine Chapel need a dedicated morning — go early to beat the crowds — followed by St. Peter's Basilica. This is the other half of Rome's must-see list, and trying to combine it with Day 2's sites in a single day is the most common overpacking mistake in a first Rome visit.

### Day 4: Rome → Florence (travel day, but a short one)

The high-speed Frecciarossa and Italo trains cover [Rome to Florence](/routes/rome-to-florence) in about 1.5 hours, with frequent departures throughout the day, so this doesn't have to eat your whole morning. Take a late-morning train, arrive in Florence in time for lunch, and spend the afternoon getting oriented — the Duomo, Ponte Vecchio, and a walk through the historic center. If you'd rather skip the station transfer and luggage juggling, a private car between the two cities covers the same distance door-to-door in roughly the same time.

### Day 5: Florence — Renaissance Florence

A full day for the Uffizi Gallery (book ahead — it sells out) and the Accademia to see Michelangelo's David, plus time in the Oltrarno neighborhood, which gets a fraction of the foot traffic of the main center. [Florence](/destinations/florence) rewards a full unhurried day more than almost any other stop on this route.

### Day 6: Florence → Venice

Trains from Florence to Venice run via Bologna and take a little over two hours on the fastest Frecciarossa services — see the [Florence to Venice route](/routes/florence-to-venice) for the specifics. Leave by mid-morning and you'll be in Venice with the whole afternoon ahead of you. Spend it simply getting lost in the calli around San Marco and the Rialto Bridge before the evening light hits the Grand Canal.

### Day 7: Venice — and departure

A final full day in [Venice](/destinations/venice): St. Mark's Basilica and the Doge's Palace in the morning, then a vaporetto ride down the Grand Canal or a wander through the quieter Dorsoduro and Cannaregio districts in the afternoon. If your flight home departs the next morning from Venice Marco Polo, stay the night; if you need to fly out of Rome or Milan, build in a travel day rather than trying to squeeze in one more.

### Pacing notes

This is a fast week by design — one real moving day out of seven (Day 4 and Day 6 are half-travel, half-sightseeing, which keeps the trip from feeling rushed). There's no slack day built in, so treat Day 1 as your buffer if a flight runs late.

### Who this itinerary suits

First-time visitors, couples, and anyone whose primary interest is art, history, and the "greatest hits" of Italy. It's also the right call if seven days is truly all you have — trying to force a fourth city into this timeframe usually means seeing all four badly rather than three well.

### How to adapt it

- **Reverse it**: Fly into Venice and out of Rome (or vice versa) to avoid backtracking, especially useful if your flights naturally route that way.
- **Swap Venice for Milan**: If fashion, design, or a Lake Como add-on interests you more than canals, replace Venice with Milan — the Florence-Milan corridor runs on the same high-speed network.
- **Add a day**: If you can stretch to 8 days, insert it in Florence as a day trip to Pisa (about an hour each way) rather than adding a fourth city.

## The 10-Day Italy Itinerary: Rome, the Amalfi Coast & the North

Ten days is enough to add real depth — either a slower pace through the same three cities, or, as built here, a genuine second region: the Amalfi Coast. This version trades a little bit of Rome-Florence-Venice efficiency for a coastal change of scenery that a 7-day trip can't accommodate.

### Days 1–3: Rome

Follow the same structure as the 7-day plan: an easy arrival day, a full day for ancient Rome (Colosseum, Forum, Palatine Hill), and a full day for Vatican City. Three days lets you also fit in the Borghese Gallery or a slower food-and-market morning in Testaccio or the Jewish Ghetto, something the 7-day version has no room for.

### Day 4: Rome → Sorrento (via Pompeii)

This is the itinerary's real travel day. A private transfer on the [Rome to Sorrento](/routes/rome-to-sorrento) route takes roughly 3 to 3.5 hours direct, but the standard and much better use of the day is to break the drive with a stop at Pompeii, which sits almost exactly on the route. Budget the better part of the day for this leg — it's the trade-off for reaching the coast at all. Doing this by private chauffeur rather than train-plus-local-transport removes the need to change modes of transport twice (train to Naples, then a separate transfer to Sorrento).

### Day 5: Amalfi Coast day trip

From a Sorrento base, spend the day on the [Amalfi Coast](/destinations/amalfi-coast) — Positano and the town of Amalfi are the two anchor stops, connected by the coastal road that's arguably the scenic highlight of the whole trip. Given the road's hairpin turns and summer traffic, this is a day to hire a driver rather than self-navigate.

### Day 6: Sorrento — Capri or rest day

Use this as a flex day: a boat trip to Capri, more time in Sorrento itself, or simply a slower morning before tomorrow's travel. Building in one unscheduled day around the halfway point is what keeps a 10-day trip from feeling like a checklist.

### Day 7: Sorrento → Florence

The most efficient way north is back through Naples to catch the high-speed rail line — the Naples-to-Florence run takes a little under 3 hours on the fastest trains. Combined with the transfer from Sorrento to Naples, plan on this being a full travel day; arrive in Florence in the evening and save sightseeing for tomorrow.

### Day 8: Florence

A full day for the Uffizi, the Accademia, and the Duomo — the same core sights as the 7-day version, but without the same time pressure since you're staying two nights before moving on.

### Day 9: Tuscany day trip

Take a day trip into the Tuscan countryside, ideally combining Siena with a smaller hill town like San Gimignano or a stop in the Chianti wine region. Siena alone is about 1 to 1.5 hours from Florence by road — see the [Florence to Siena route](/routes/florence-to-siena) — which makes a single-day loop comfortable without feeling rushed.

### Day 10: Florence → Venice, and departure

Take a mid-morning train on the Florence to Venice line (just over two hours) and spend the afternoon and evening in Venice before departing the following day, or use this as a half-day in Venice if your flight is that evening. If you can, add an eleventh day here rather than rushing the finale — Venice deserves more than an afternoon.

### Pacing notes

This itinerary has two real travel days (Day 4 and Day 7) bookending the coastal stretch, plus one built-in flex day (Day 6). It's a fuller pace than the 7-day trip but not a rushed one — the trade-off for adding an entire region is accepting two half-lost travel days.

### Who this itinerary suits

Repeat visitors to Italy who've already done Rome-Florence-Venice, or first-timers who specifically want coastal scenery alongside the classic art cities. It suits couples and small groups well; families with young children may find the Amalfi Coast's driving and stairs more demanding than the flatter city-based days.

### How to adapt it

- **Swap the coast for a slower north**: If the Amalfi Coast doesn't appeal, replace Days 4–6 with a second Tuscany-based stretch — a night in Siena itself, or a countryside stay — and keep the trip entirely within central and northern Italy.
- **Extend the Amalfi stretch**: Add a day and use it for Naples itself, which most transfers pass right through without stopping.
- **Reverse the route**: Start in Venice, work down to Florence and Rome, then fly out of Naples instead of doubling back north — this can save an entire travel day if your flights support it.

## The 14-Day Italy Itinerary: The Complete Grand Tour

Two weeks is enough to cover Italy's south, center, and north without any single day feeling like a sprint. This version runs Rome, the Amalfi Coast, Florence and Tuscany, Venice, and finishes in Milan with a Lake Como extension — a genuine loop rather than an out-and-back.

### Days 1–3: Rome

Arrival day, then two full sightseeing days split between ancient Rome (Colosseum, Forum, Palatine Hill) and Vatican City, exactly as in the shorter itineraries. With 14 days available, consider adding a half-day for the Borghese Gallery gardens or an evening food tour — Rome is the one city on this route that can comfortably absorb extra time.

### Day 4: Rome → Sorrento

As in the 10-day plan, take a private transfer down via Pompeii — the Rome to Sorrento leg runs about 3 to 3.5 hours direct, longer with the Pompeii stop built in. This is the longest single travel day of the trip.

### Day 5: Amalfi Coast

A full day covering Positano and Amalfi along the coastal road, best done with a private driver given the narrow switchbacks and heavy midday traffic in peak season.

### Day 6: Capri

With extra days available, dedicate one fully to Capri — a boat crossing from Sorrento, the Blue Grotto (conditions permitting), and the town of Anacapri. This is the kind of day the 10-day version has to compress into "Capri or rest"; here it gets its own slot.

### Day 7: Sorrento → Florence

Transfer back through Naples to the high-speed line for the run north to Florence, roughly 3 hours on the fastest trains. Plan for a full travel day and arrive in the evening.

### Day 8: Florence

A full day for the Uffizi, Accademia, and the Duomo complex.

### Day 9: Tuscany day trip

A day trip to Siena and the surrounding countryside, or split the day between Siena and Pisa if seeing the Leaning Tower matters to you — Pisa is under an hour from Florence by train, making a half-day visit realistic.

### Day 10: Florence → Venice

Take the Florence to Venice train (a little over two hours) with a mid-morning departure, arriving with the afternoon free for a first look at St. Mark's Square and the Rialto Bridge.

### Day 11: Venice

A full day in Venice: the Doge's Palace and St. Mark's Basilica in the morning, then the quieter Dorsoduro and Cannaregio districts, or a vaporetto trip out to Murano and Burano, in the afternoon.

### Day 12: Venice → Milan (via Verona)

Rather than a direct run, break the Venice to Milan train journey with a stop in Verona, which sits roughly midway and is easily worth two to three hours for the Arena and Piazza delle Erbe. Continue to Milan in the afternoon; the direct Milan-Venice segment alone runs about 2.5 hours on the fastest trains, so budget the better part of the day once you factor in the Verona stop.

### Day 13: Lake Como day trip

From a Milan base, take the short trip out to Lake Como — the train from Milan runs as little as 40 minutes to Como's lakefront station, making this an easy half-day or full-day excursion depending on whether you also want to reach Bellagio by boat. See the [Milan to Lake Como route](/routes/milan-to-lake-como) for details. Return to Milan in the evening.

### Day 14: Milan — and departure

Use the morning for [Milan](/destinations/milan) itself — the Duomo, the Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II, and, if you book ahead, Leonardo's Last Supper — before departing from Milan Malpensa or Linate.

### Pacing notes

Three real travel days (Days 4, 7, and 12) are spread out across the two weeks rather than clustered, and two dedicated day-trip days (Tuscany, Lake Como) plus the Capri day give the trip real breathing room. This is a sustainable pace even for travelers who don't love constant movement — no more than three or four consecutive nights in one place before a change of scenery, but never two travel days back to back.

### Who this itinerary suits

Repeat visitors doing a comprehensive first "grand tour," or first-time visitors with two full weeks who want to see all of Italy's major regions rather than picking one. It works well for families who can handle a moderate amount of travel, and for mixed-interest groups since the route balances art and history (Rome, Florence) with scenery and leisure (Amalfi Coast, Lake Como).

### How to adapt it

- **Drop the south, add the north**: If two weeks feels like a lot for both coasts, cut Days 4–6 (Sorrento/Amalfi/Capri) and replace them with additional nights in Tuscany or an extension to Bologna, reachable easily from Florence, for a food-focused detour.
- **Reverse the loop**: Start in Milan, head to Lake Como and Venice first, then work south through Florence to Rome and finish on the Amalfi Coast before flying out of Naples — this avoids the backtrack through Naples that the Rome-first version requires.
- **Add a coastal night**: If Amalfi Coast scenery is a priority, add a night actually staying in Positano or Sorrento rather than day-tripping from a single base, which removes the round-trip driving on Day 5.

## Comparing the Three Itineraries at a Glance

| | 7 Days | 10 Days | 14 Days |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Destinations covered** | Rome, Florence, Venice | Rome, Sorrento/Amalfi Coast, Florence, Tuscany, Venice | Rome, Sorrento/Amalfi Coast/Capri, Florence, Tuscany, Venice, Verona, Milan, Lake Como |
| **Regions** | Central and Northeast Italy | Central, South, and Northeast Italy | Central, South, and Northern Italy (full loop) |
| **Real travel days** | 2 | 2 (plus 1 flex day) | 3 (plus 2 day-trip days) |
| **Pace** | Fast — one city every 2–3 days | Moderate — includes one built-in rest/flex day | Sustainable — no back-to-back travel days |
| **Best for** | First-timers, art/history focus, short trips | Repeat visitors, coastal scenery + classics | Comprehensive first "grand tour," mixed-interest groups |
| **Families with young kids** | Works well — minimal transfers | Doable but Amalfi Coast driving is demanding | Best of the three — pace allows for rest |

## General Guidance for Adapting Any of These Itineraries

A few adjustments apply regardless of which itinerary you start from:

- **Reversing the route** almost never costs you anything and can save real time if it better matches your inbound and outbound flights — none of these routes are meaningfully faster in one direction than the other.
- **Swapping a city** works best when you swap for something on the same rail corridor. Venice and Milan sit on the same line as Florence, so trading one for the other doesn't add a travel day; adding a city off that spine (say, a stop in the Dolomites) usually does.
- **Adding a day** is almost always better spent as a day trip from an existing base — Pisa or Siena from Florence, Lake Como from Milan — than as a new hotel stay, since it avoids the overhead of packing and checking in again.
- **Coastal or lake extensions** (Amalfi Coast, Lake Como, Cinque Terre) are the easiest additions to graft onto any of these plans, but they're also the ones most worth arranging as a [private transfer](/city-to-city-transfers) rather than public transport — the roads involved are scenic but slow, and a driver who knows the route saves real time over self-navigating with a rental car.
- **If you're combining more than three cities** on any timeline shorter than 10 days, treat that as a signal to cut one rather than push through — the itineraries above are built around the idea that a city seen properly for two days is worth more than three cities seen for a day each.

For any of these routes, arranging transport in advance — whether single-leg city-to-city transfers or a fully planned [private tour of Italy](/italy-private-tours) — removes the logistics from the trip so the itinerary above is something you actually follow rather than something you spend half your vacation rebooking.
