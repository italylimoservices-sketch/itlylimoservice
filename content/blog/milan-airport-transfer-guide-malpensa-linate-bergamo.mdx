Milan is one of the few Italian cities where booking a flight "to Milan" doesn't actually tell you much. Three separate airports serve the metropolitan area, and they sit in very different places relative to the city center. Land at the wrong assumption about which one you're using, and you can end up planning your **Milan airport transfer** around the wrong distance entirely.

This guide walks through all three airports — Malpensa, Linate, and Bergamo — so you can figure out which one applies to your trip and roughly what to expect once you land.

## Why Milan Has Three Airports

Most major cities have one primary airport, maybe two. Milan has three because of how air traffic in northern Italy developed over the decades: a large international hub built to handle long-haul and high-volume traffic, a smaller in-town airport kept open mainly for shorter European routes and business travel, and a third airport in a neighboring city that grew rapidly once low-cost carriers started using it heavily.

None of the three is "the Milan airport" in the way, say, Fiumicino is unambiguously Rome's main airport. Depending on your airline, route, and ticket price, you could land at any of them.

## Milan Malpensa (MXP): The Main International Gateway

Malpensa is Milan's largest airport and the one most long-haul and international travelers will use. If you're flying in from North America, Asia, or on most major intercontinental routes, Malpensa is very likely your airport.

It's also the airport furthest from central Milan. Malpensa sits roughly 50 km from the city center, and the journey into Milan typically takes 45 to 60 minutes, depending on traffic and time of day. That's a meaningful distance to plan around, especially if you're arriving after a long flight and just want to reach your hotel.

Malpensa has two terminals, handles a large volume of daily traffic, and is generally the busiest of the three airports for international arrivals. For a closer look at getting from Malpensa into the city, the [Milan Malpensa airport transfer](/airport-transfers/milan-malpensa) page covers the route in more detail.

## Milan Linate (LIN): Close-In and Business-Friendly

Linate is the airport many business travelers and short-haul European flights use, largely because of one major advantage: it's close. Linate sits only about 8 km from central Milan, and the drive into the city usually takes 15 to 25 minutes, depending on traffic.

That proximity makes Linate a popular choice for travelers who want to minimize the time between landing and getting into meetings, or simply don't want to spend an hour in transit after a short flight from elsewhere in Europe. It's smaller and generally less overwhelming than Malpensa, with a more straightforward layout.

If your itinerary shows LIN as the airport code, you're in a considerably better position time-wise than someone flying into Malpensa or Bergamo. More detail on this airport specifically is available on the [Milan Linate airport transfer](/airport-transfers/milan-linate) page.

## Bergamo Airport (BGY): Budget Carriers and a Longer Drive

Bergamo Airport, sometimes listed as Orio al Serio, is the airport most associated with low-cost and charter carriers. Many travelers book a flight that shows "Milan Bergamo" as the destination without fully registering that Bergamo is actually a separate city, roughly 45 km from central Milan.

The drive from Bergamo into Milan generally takes 50 to 65 minutes, depending on traffic — making it the farthest of the three airports from the city center, even a bit further than Malpensa in typical travel time. Travelers who booked a low-cost fare specifically because it was cheap sometimes don't realize the distance until they're standing at arrivals trying to work out transportation. ## Comparing the Three Airports

| Airport | Distance to Central Milan | Typical Drive Time | Common For |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Malpensa (MXP) | ~50 km | 45–60 minutes | Long-haul and international flights |
| Linate (LIN) | ~8 km | 15–25 minutes | Business travel, short-haul European routes |
| Bergamo (BGY) | ~45 km | 50–65 minutes | Low-cost and charter carriers |

All three figures can shift with traffic, time of day, and road conditions, so treat them as general planning ranges rather than guarantees.

## How to Figure Out Which Airport Applies to You

The simplest way to know which airport you're using is to check the three-letter airport code on your boarding pass or itinerary: MXP for Malpensa, LIN for Linate, and BGY for Bergamo. It's an easy detail to overlook, particularly when a booking site simply lists the destination as "Milan."

It's also worth checking before you book, not just before you fly. If you're comparing two similarly priced flights and one lands at Linate while the other lands at Bergamo, that difference alone could mean roughly 40 extra minutes of travel time in each direction, which adds up if your trip is short.

A few other things worth confirming ahead of time:

- Which terminal you're arriving at, particularly at Malpensa, which has two
- Whether your onward plans involve a hotel in central Milan or somewhere else in the metropolitan area, such as near [Porta Nuova](/destinations/milan) or the Fiera Milano exhibition centre
- Whether you're connecting to another destination, such as [Lake Como](/destinations/lake-como) or [Turin](/destinations/turin), where the airport you land at can affect your onward route

## Choosing an Airport When You Actually Have Options

Sometimes you don't have a choice — your route only flies into one of the three. But if you're booking a trip with some flexibility, particularly on a connecting itinerary through another European city, it's worth checking whether an alternative routing lands you at Linate instead of Malpensa or Bergamo.

The fare difference between airports can go either way, and it's rarely large enough to be the deciding factor on its own. What's more useful is thinking about the shape of your trip. A short business visit with a tight schedule benefits disproportionately from Linate's proximity. A longer holiday where you're arriving with the whole afternoon free is far less sensitive to whether your transfer takes 20 minutes or an hour.

It's also worth thinking about what happens after you land. If your onward plans involve a day trip to [Lake Como](/destinations/lake-como) or a drive toward [Turin](/destinations/turin), the airport you use can shift your total travel time in ways that aren't obvious from the flight search results alone.

## Seasonal and Time-of-Day Considerations

None of the drive-time figures above are fixed. Traffic around all three airports tends to build during weekday commuting hours, and Malpensa in particular can see longer queues and busier roads around major holiday travel periods. Bergamo's roads can also feel the effect of a wave of low-cost flights landing within a short window of each other, since a cluster of arrivals means a cluster of ground transport demand all at once.

Arriving very early in the morning or late at night generally means lighter traffic but also fewer transport options actively running, particularly for public transit. If your flight lands outside normal daytime hours, it's worth checking in advance what's actually available rather than assuming every option listed here operates around the clock.

## Getting Into the City From Any of the Three

However you arrive, you'll generally have similar categories of transport available at each airport — trains where applicable, taxis, shared shuttles, and private transfers. What differs most is simply the distance you're covering and how predictable you want that journey to be.

For travelers who'd rather not work out train connections, taxi lines, or luggage logistics after a long flight, arranging an [airport transfer](/airport-transfers) in advance takes that uncertainty out of the equation regardless of which of the three airports you land at. A driver who already knows the route, and who's tracking your flight rather than a fixed clock time, can make the difference between a smooth start to your trip and a stressful one.

## Final Thoughts

Milan's three airports aren't interchangeable, and the airport code on your ticket matters more than most travelers expect. Malpensa handles the bulk of international arrivals but sits furthest out in terms of practical travel time alongside Bergamo; Linate offers a much shorter hop into the city; and Bergamo, despite its low-cost appeal, comes with the longest drive of the three.

Whichever airport your flight uses, knowing the distance and likely travel time ahead of arrival makes it much easier to plan the rest of your day — and to arrange the right kind of transportation for it.
