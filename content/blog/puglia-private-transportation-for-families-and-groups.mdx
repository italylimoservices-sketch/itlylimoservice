A trip to Puglia with eight, ten, or twelve people — grandparents, parents, kids, maybe an extended family reunion or a group of friends splitting a villa — runs into a logistics question that smaller trips never have to face: how does everyone actually move through the day together, between towns that weren't built with large groups in mind?

## The Group-Size Problem Is Different From the Family Problem

A couple traveling with young kids deals mostly with pacing and patience. A group of eight or more deals with something more structural: no single standard vehicle in most private fleets comfortably seats more than about seven people, which means a group above that size is, by definition, traveling in more than one vehicle whether anyone planned for it or not.

That's not a problem exactly, but it's a different kind of planning than a couple or a small family needs to do. The questions shift from "will the kids be okay in the car" to "how do we keep everyone together, arriving and leaving at the same time, without one group waiting twenty minutes for the other at every stop."

## Sizing Vehicles Honestly

The starting point is an honest headcount — not just how many people, but how they naturally split. A multi-generational group often has a genuine logic to how it divides: grandparents and younger kids in one vehicle, teenagers and adults in another, for instance. Naming that split when requesting a quote, rather than just giving a total number, helps get vehicles proposed that actually match how the group wants to travel, not just how many seats are needed in total.

For reference, the relevant capacities are:

- A [luxury SUV](/fleet/luxury-suv) seats up to five passengers and holds up to four suitcases
- An [executive van](/fleet/executive-van) or [luxury van](/fleet/luxury-van) seats up to seven passengers and holds up to six suitcases

A group of nine, for example, doesn't fit any single vehicle in that list — it needs two vehicles, most likely a van plus an SUV, or two vans if luggage volume calls for it. Working that out before arrival, rather than at the curb, is what keeps the first day of the trip from starting with confusion.

## Coordinating Multiple Vehicles for One Day

The harder part of a multi-generational or large-group trip usually isn't the vehicle count — it's keeping everyone moving through the same day together when the group is split across more than one car. A few things make a real difference here:

**Shared departure and arrival times.** When two vehicles are involved, it helps to plan around the same windows rather than letting each one run independently, so the group doesn't end up badly staggered at a destination that has limited stopping room — Alberobello and Ostuni's old towns in particular don't have much space for multiple vehicles to idle while waiting for each other.

**One point of contact for the day's plan.** If the itinerary shifts — a stop gets added, timing moves — it's far simpler for one person to communicate that once than for two separate vehicles to be working from slightly different information.

**Clarity on where the group actually meets.** In towns with restricted or narrow-street access, the realistic drop-off point for a van and for an SUV might not be identical. Deciding in advance where the group reconvenes avoids everyone wandering in slightly different directions looking for each other.

## Keeping a Group Together Through Puglia's Historic Centers

This matters more in Puglia than in a lot of other places, because towns like Alberobello, Ostuni, and Lecce have old centers with real limits on vehicle access — narrow lanes, pedestrian zones, and drop-off points that aren't always right next to each other. A group of twelve arriving in two vehicles may genuinely be dropped at two different points a short walk apart, simply because that's where each vehicle can actually stop. Knowing this ahead of time, rather than being surprised by it, makes the walk-in feel like part of the plan instead of a mistake.

## What to Include in a Group Booking Request

A request for a larger or multi-generational group benefits from more detail upfront than a simple two-person transfer would need:

- Total headcount, and how the group naturally splits if it needs to travel in more than one vehicle
- Ages of any children, and whether car seats are needed — availability should always be confirmed directly rather than assumed
- Luggage volume, since a large group often means more bags than vehicle capacity alone would suggest
- Whether the group needs to move together all day or is comfortable splitting up and reconvening at set points
- Any mobility considerations for older travelers, particularly relevant given the walking that Puglia's old towns often require

## Why More Detail Early Saves Time Later

A group request with this level of detail tends to get a workable proposal back on the first try — the right number of vehicles, sized appropriately, with a sensible plan for how they move together. A vaguer request, just a headcount and a date, usually comes back with follow-up questions before anything can actually be confirmed. For a big family trip or a reunion with a fixed arrival date, that extra round of back-and-forth is exactly the kind of delay worth avoiding by giving the full picture the first time. ## A Villa or Masseria Base Changes the Math Too

Many larger Puglia groups base themselves in a countryside villa or a converted masseria rather than a hotel in town, since that kind of property tends to suit a big family group better than splitting across several hotel rooms. This adds a layer worth planning for: the property itself may sit down a rural lane that a large van handles differently than a sedan would, and a group staying somewhere more remote is also starting every single day's outing from the same point, rather than from a central town location.

Mentioning the accommodation type in a booking request — villa, masseria, or hotel — helps the operator think through the actual access involved, especially for a group large enough to need more than one vehicle meeting at the same rural pickup point.

## Balancing Group Time With Flexibility

Not every large group wants to move as one unit for an entire trip, and there's nothing wrong with that. Some multi-generational trips work best with everyone together for arrival, departure, and perhaps one or two shared excursion days, while other days split naturally — grandparents preferring a slower town visit while younger members of the group take a longer countryside day. Private transportation actually makes this easier than it sounds, since two vehicles can run entirely separate plans for part of a trip and recombine for a shared dinner or a shared return to the airport. The key, again, is naming that structure at the booking stage rather than assuming it will sort itself out once everyone's in Puglia.

## The Bigger Picture

Puglia is genuinely well suited to a big family or group trip — there's enough variety across the towns and countryside to keep different ages and interests satisfied without anyone feeling dragged along. The group-size logistics just need a bit more deliberate planning than a couple or small family would need, mostly around vehicle count and keeping everyone synced through towns that don't always make it easy. Get that part right at the booking stage, and the size of the group stops being a logistics problem and goes back to being what it should be: more people to enjoy the trip with.
