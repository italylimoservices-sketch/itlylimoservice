Leaving Italy involves more moving parts than most travelers expect — not because the process is complicated, but because a few of its steps (the VAT refund chief among them) have to happen in a specific order, before others, or they don't happen at all. Miss the customs desk before you check your bag, and there's no fixing it once you're airside. This guide walks through the departure-day sequence at Italy's major airports, step by step, from the moment you decide when to leave for the airport to the moment you board.

It covers the airport-side process only. If you're still deciding how to get to the airport itself — taxi, train, or private transfer — that comparison lives in a separate guide; here we start from the terminal doors.

## How Much Buffer Time to Leave Before Your Flight

Italy's main airports don't publish a single universal number, because arrival times depend on the airline, the route, and how busy the terminal is that day. But the official guidance from Aeroporti di Roma (which runs Fiumicino) gives a solid baseline that holds up across Milan Malpensa and Venice Marco Polo as well:

- **Domestic and Schengen flights** (Italy to another Schengen-area country): arrive at least **2 hours** before departure.
- **Extra-Schengen international flights** (the US, UK, Canada, and other non-Schengen destinations): arrive at least **3 hours** before departure.
- **During peak hours** — typically 5–8 AM and 4–7 PM — add another 30 minutes to both figures, since security lines build quickly and terminal walking distances at Fiumicino and Malpensa are considerable.
- **Checking a bag** adds time on top of this, since you'll need to queue at a check-in counter or bag-drop desk rather than heading straight to security.

If you're claiming a VAT refund on unused goods in your checked luggage, build in extra time before check-in — the customs step has to happen first, and queues at the customs desk vary widely by time of day.

One thing that removes a layer of uncertainty from this calculation entirely: a [pre-booked airport transfer](/airport-transfers) means you're not estimating taxi availability or train frequency on top of everything else — your pickup time is fixed against your flight, and the driver adjusts for traffic. That's especially useful heading into Rome or Milan, where road conditions on any given morning are hard to predict from a hotel room.

### Departure-Day Timeline (Example: International Flight)

| Time Before Departure | What to Do |
|---|---|
| 3–3.5 hours | Leave for the airport; arrive and locate check-in |
| 2.5–3 hours | Complete check-in / bag drop; visit customs desk first if claiming a VAT refund on checked items |
| 2–2.5 hours | Clear security screening |
| 1.5–2 hours | Passport control (non-EU passengers) |
| 1–1.5 hours | Duty-free browsing, final VAT refund pickup if using an airport kiosk |
| 30–45 min | Proceed to gate; boarding typically begins |
| 15–20 min | Gate closes (varies by airline — always confirm on your boarding pass) |

## Online Check-In and Baggage Drop

Nearly every airline serving Italy's major airports supports online or app-based check-in, typically opening 24–48 hours before departure. Doing this in advance saves a queue at the airport and, for many airlines, is required to avoid a check-in fee at the counter.

At the airport itself:

- **Bag-drop desks** for passengers who already checked in online usually close **around 40–60 minutes** before a domestic or Schengen departure, and **closer to 60–90 minutes** before a long-haul international flight — but this is airline-specific, so check your confirmation email rather than assuming.
- **Full check-in counters** (for anyone who hasn't checked in online) close earlier and often require more time, particularly if you need to sort out seat assignments, excess baggage, or documentation.
- Self-service bag-drop kiosks are increasingly common at Fiumicino and Malpensa and can be faster than staffed counters outside of peak hours.

If you're departing with items you plan to claim a VAT refund on and any of them are in your checked bag, do not go to the check-in desk first. That's the single most common mistake travelers make on departure day, and it's irreversible once your bag has disappeared onto the belt.

## The VAT Refund Process for Non-EU Residents

This is the step with the most room for error, largely because it has to happen in the right order and at the right desk. Here's how it actually works.

### Who Qualifies, and How Much You Need to Spend

The refund applies to travelers who are **residents of a non-EU country**, buying goods for personal use that will leave the EU in their luggage. As of the current threshold, you need to spend **more than €70** (specifically, €70.01) **in a single store, on a single receipt**, to qualify — smaller individual purchases don't accumulate toward this unless made in one transaction at one retailer.

### Shopping: Look for the Tax-Free Logo

Not every shop offers tax-free processing, even if they're happy to sell to tourists. Look for a **tax-free shopping logo** displayed at the register (Global Blue, Planet, and similar operators are the most common in Italy), or simply ask before you buy. The store will issue you a tax-free form or digital invoice at the time of purchase — you'll need your passport at checkout to have it filled in correctly.

### Getting the Customs Stamp — Before You Check Your Bag

This is the step that trips people up. Italy uses a digital customs system called **OTELLO** (Online Tax refund at Exit: Light Lane Optimization) at its main airports, including Rome Fiumicino, Milan Malpensa, Venice, and Florence.

The critical rule: **if the goods you're claiming a refund on are in your checked luggage, you must go to the customs desk (or the tax-free operator's kiosk) before you check that bag in.** Customs officers may ask to physically inspect the purchased items to confirm they're unused and match the invoice. If you've already checked your bag and can't produce the goods, the stamp will be refused — there's no workaround at that point.

If everything you're claiming is in your carry-on, you can generally complete this step after security, closer to your gate, since you'll have the goods with you throughout.

At the OTELLO desk or kiosk, here's what happens:

1. You present your passport, boarding pass, and tax-free invoice(s).
2. The system pulls up your invoice data and runs an automated risk check.
3. You're directed to either a **green channel** (approved instantly, digital stamp issued, no physical check needed) or a **red channel** (an officer inspects the goods in person before stamping).

### Getting Your Money Back

Once stamped, the refund itself is processed either:

- **In cash**, at a tax-free operator's airport counter (often with a small service fee deducted).
- **Back to your original card**, processed after you've left, which can take one to several weeks.
- **Via a self-service kiosk**, where available, which scans your stamped form and processes the refund automatically.

Keep the stamped documentation until the refund is confirmed — for card refunds in particular, you may need to post the physical stamped form back to the retailer or tax-free operator if it wasn't fully processed at the airport kiosk. Generally, the purchased goods need to leave the EU within three months of the invoice date, so this isn't something to put off if you're extending a trip into other Schengen countries afterward.

## Security Screening

The **standard EU rule** for liquids, gels, and aerosols in carry-on luggage applies at most Italian airports: containers no larger than **100ml each**, all packed together in a single transparent, resealable bag of **one liter or less**.

That said, several Italian airports have rolled out **next-generation CT scanners** for cabin baggage that can see through liquids without requiring them to be bagged or limited to 100ml:

- **Milan Linate, Bologna, and Milan Malpensa Terminal 1** now allow **up to 2 liters per container** in hand luggage, at all security lanes, for all passengers.
- **Rome Fiumicino** allows liquids over 100ml for most passengers — but **if you're flying to the US or Israel**, the standard 100ml restriction still applies to your flight regardless of the airport's scanner capability.
- **Turin Caselle and Milan Malpensa Terminal 2** only extend the relaxed limits to passengers using the **fast-track security lane**; standard lanes there still enforce the 100ml rule.
- **Venice Marco Polo** has not been confirmed as upgraded at the time of writing, so pack liquids to the standard 100ml/1-liter-bag rule if departing from there.

Given how unevenly this is rolled out — even within the same airport, depending on terminal or lane — the safest approach is to pack to the standard 100ml rule unless you've specifically confirmed your departure terminal has the upgraded scanners. If departing from [Rome Fiumicino](/airport-transfers/rome-fiumicino) or [Milan Malpensa](/airport-transfers/milan-malpensa), it's worth checking which terminal your flight uses before assuming the relaxed rule applies.

Beyond liquids, standard rules apply: laptops and large electronics typically need to come out of bags for screening (unless using a lane with CT scanning, where they can often stay in), and belts, jackets, and pocket contents go through the tray as usual.

## Passport Control for Departure

How this works depends on your nationality:

- **EU/EEA/Swiss citizens** generally use automated e-gates or a dedicated EU lane, with minimal formality.
- **Non-EU nationals** go through manual or automated passport control for an exit check.

An important recent change: the EU's **Entry/Exit System (EES)** became fully operational across Schengen borders, including Italy's, replacing manual passport stamping for non-EU short-stay travelers with **digital, biometric registration** — your entry and exit are now recorded electronically (facial image and fingerprints on first use) rather than via an ink stamp in your passport. In practice, this means you should still expect a passport control checkpoint on departure, but don't be surprised if you no longer receive a physical exit stamp the way earlier trips may have involved. Automated self-service gates using this system are increasingly available at Italy's larger airports, which can speed up the process considerably compared to a staffed desk.

Keep your boarding pass and passport together and accessible through this stage — you'll present both.

## Duty-Free Shopping

Once through security and passport control, duty-free shopping areas at Fiumicino, Malpensa, and Marco Polo offer the usual range of spirits, perfume, cosmetics, and Italian food products. A few practical notes:

- If you're connecting through another EU airport before a final non-EU destination, be mindful of liquid restrictions on that connection — duty-free liquids bought in Italy are typically sealed in tamper-evident bags with your receipt, which satisfies most transfer security checks, but rules can vary.
- Duty-free purchases are separate from the VAT refund process described above — they're already sold tax-free at the point of sale, so there's no separate refund step required for them.
- Prices aren't always cheaper than in town, particularly for Italian specialty goods; it's worth comparing against city prices if you're not pressed for time.

## Gate and Boarding Procedures

Gate information typically appears on airport screens 30–45 minutes before departure, sometimes later at busier hubs. Once your gate is posted:

- Head there promptly — Fiumicino and Malpensa are large airports, and the walk from the shopping and dining areas to a remote gate (particularly ones requiring a bus transfer to the aircraft) can take longer than it looks on the board.
- Boarding usually begins **30–40 minutes** before scheduled departure, though this varies by airline and aircraft size.
- Gates typically **close 15–20 minutes** before departure — after that, even passengers physically at the airport can be denied boarding. Airlines are not obligated to hold a flight for a late passenger, so treat the posted gate-closing time as firm.
- Have your boarding pass (digital or printed) and passport ready to present again at the gate.

## Final Pre-Flight Checklist

Before you leave the lounge, duty-free area, or gate seating for boarding, run through this:

- **Documents**: passport (with sufficient validity for your destination), boarding pass, any visa or entry documentation needed on arrival.
- **VAT refund**: customs stamp obtained *before* checking any relevant luggage, and refund collected or submitted via kiosk/counter if you wanted cash or immediate processing.
- **Electronics**: laptop, tablet, and power banks packed where they're easy to remove for security if needed; power banks kept in carry-on only (never checked luggage) per standard airline safety rules.
- **Liquids**: packed to the 100ml/1-liter-bag standard unless you've confirmed your specific terminal has upgraded scanners.
- **Chargers and cables**: consolidated somewhere accessible rather than buried in checked luggage, in case of a security recheck or a layover.
- **Local currency and small change**: used up or exchanged if you won't be returning soon.
- **Confirmation of onward transport at your destination**, if applicable, since time zones and long-haul arrival schedules can catch travelers off guard after departure.

Booking your route to the airport through a [chauffeur service](/chauffeur-service) that tracks your flight and adjusts pickup timing removes one more variable from a day that already has several — leaving more of your attention for the parts of departure day that actually require it, like the customs desk and your gate time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How early should I arrive at the airport for a flight from Italy?**
At least 2 hours before a domestic or Schengen flight, and at least 3 hours before an extra-Schengen international flight, per official guidance from Rome's airport authority — add 30 minutes during peak morning and evening hours.

**Do I need to get my VAT refund form stamped before or after check-in?**
Before, if the goods are in your checked luggage. You must show the unused items to customs before your bag disappears onto the belt, or the stamp can be refused. If everything is in your carry-on, you can do this after security instead.

**What's the minimum purchase amount for a VAT refund in Italy?**
You need to spend more than €70 (€70.01) in a single store on a single receipt to qualify for tax-free shopping as a non-EU resident.

**Can I still bring liquids over 100ml in my carry-on at Italian airports?**
At some airports and terminals — including Milan Linate, Bologna, and Malpensa Terminal 1 — new CT scanners allow up to 2 liters per container. Rome Fiumicino allows it too, except for flights to the US or Israel. Where scanners haven't been upgraded, including Venice Marco Polo, the standard 100ml rule still applies.

**Will I get a passport stamp when I leave Italy?**
Not necessarily. The EU's Entry/Exit System now records non-EU travelers' entries and exits digitally and biometrically at Schengen borders, replacing manual ink stamps in many cases. You'll still go through a passport control checkpoint, but the process is increasingly automated.

**How long before my flight does the gate close?**
Typically 15–20 minutes before scheduled departure, though this varies by airline. Boarding itself usually starts 30–40 minutes before departure.

**Can I get my VAT refund at a kiosk instead of a counter?**
Yes, self-service refund kiosks are available at several major Italian airports and can process a stamped tax-free form directly onto a card, though counters with staff remain the option for cash refunds.

**Should I book a transfer to the airport in advance?**
It's not required, but it removes a layer of uncertainty from an already time-sensitive day — a [pre-arranged transfer](/airport-transfers) fixes your pickup time against your flight rather than leaving it to taxi availability or traffic on the day.
