Print this out, pin it to the fridge, or save it to your phone — this is the checklist version of Italy trip prep, organized purely by timing. No theory, no "here's how to think about planning a trip" — just what to do and when to do it, from the day you book your flights to the moment you're standing on a train platform wondering if you need to validate your ticket (you do — more on that below).

Each section below covers one window of time. Work through them in order as your trip approaches, and use the summary table near the end as a one-page overview.

## 2–3+ Months Before Departure

This is the window for anything with a lead time — visas, flights, and the handful of Italian attractions that sell out weeks or months in advance.

- **Check your passport's expiry date.** For Schengen countries, including Italy, your passport must be valid for at least 3 months beyond your planned departure date from the Schengen area, and it generally must have been issued within the last 10 years. If it's close, renew now — passport offices can take weeks.
- **Check visa/ETIAS requirements for your nationality.** Many non-EU nationals (including US, UK, Canadian, and Australian citizens) can currently enter visa-free for short stays. Note that ETIAS, the EU's pre-travel screening system for visa-exempt travelers, has been delayed multiple times and is not yet in effect — don't pay a third-party site to "apply" for it. Check your government's travel advisory page or the official EU visa portal for your specific situation closer to departure, since rules can shift.
- **Book your flights.** Compare into which airport makes sense for your itinerary (Rome Fiumicino, Milan Malpensa, Venice Marco Polo) and lock in dates.
- **Book your accommodation**, especially in Rome, Florence, Venice, and the Amalfi Coast in peak season (May–September), where good properties sell out early.
- **Reserve major timed-entry attractions.** Book ahead:
  - Uffizi Gallery and Accademia (Florence) — sell out days to weeks ahead in high season
  - Vatican Museums and Sistine Chapel — book skip-the-line slots well in advance, especially spring/summer
  - Colosseum, Roman Forum, and Palatine Hill (with any underground/arena floor add-on)
  - Last Supper (Milan) — extremely limited daily capacity, often booked out weeks ahead
- **Consider travel insurance.** Compare policies for trip cancellation, medical coverage, and baggage — decide before cancellation windows on flights/hotels start tightening.
- **Sketch your intercity route** so you know which cities need a private [chauffeur service](/chauffeur-service) versus a train — this shapes your bookings in the next phase.

## 3–4 Weeks Before Departure

Now you're locking in the logistics that connect the pieces of your itinerary.

- **Book intercity transport.** Reserve high-speed trains (Trenitalia/Italo) for popular routes like Rome–Florence or Florence–Venice — prices rise and preferred departure times fill up as the date approaches. For groups, families, or door-to-door comfort with luggage, arrange [city-to-city transfers](/city-to-city-transfers) instead of navigating stations.
- **Reserve any private tours or transfers.** If you want a driver for a day in the Tuscan countryside, an [Italy private tour](/italy-private-tours), or transfers between hotels and attractions, confirm dates now while your preferred time slots are still open.
- **Book special-occasion restaurants.** Well-known trattorias, anniversary dinners, or anything with a view (Michelin-listed spots, rooftop restaurants) often need reservations 2–4 weeks out.
- **Notify your bank and credit card companies of your travel dates** to avoid fraud holds on your card mid-trip. Confirm foreign transaction fees while you're at it.
- **Sort your phone plan.** Compare your carrier's international day-pass against a local or global eSIM — EU roaming rules only benefit residents of EU/EEA countries, so most non-EU visitors will pay international rates unless they set up an eSIM or local SIM in advance. Many eSIMs can be purchased and activated online before you leave.
- **Confirm your airport pickup or arrival transfer**, particularly if you're arriving after a long overnight flight and don't want to deal with taxi queues — booking an [airport transfer](/airport-transfers) ahead of time means a driver is waiting with your name on arrival.

## 1–2 Weeks Before Departure

This is when the trip stops being abstract and starts being a suitcase.

- **Start your packing list** (layers for variable weather, comfortable walking shoes for cobblestones, one smart-casual outfit for nicer restaurants or churches with dress codes).
- **Check the weather forecast** for your specific cities and date range, and adjust what you're packing accordingly — don't rely on generic "Italy in summer" assumptions.
- **Download offline tools before you go:**
  - Offline Google Maps for each city you're visiting
  - A translation app with offline language packs
  - The Trenitalia or Italo app if you're taking trains
  - Any museum or attraction apps where your tickets will live
- **Save or print digital copies of key documents** — passport photo page, flight confirmations, hotel bookings, attraction tickets, and travel insurance policy. Keep one copy in the cloud (email yourself a PDF) and one offline in case you lose signal or battery.
- **Sort out currency.** Italy is largely card-friendly, but carry some cash (€50–100 to start) for small cafés, local markets, tips, and tourist tax at check-in (see below). Confirm your card has no foreign transaction fees, or order euros through your bank if you prefer arriving with cash in hand.
- **Reconfirm all reservations** — hotels, trains, tours, restaurants — while there's still time to fix a mistake.

## 2–3 Days Before Departure

The home stretch — final confirmations and logistics.

- **Check in for your flight** as soon as the airline's window opens (usually 24–48 hours before departure) and save your boarding pass to your phone's wallet app.
- **Check your baggage allowance** for both your international flight and any budget intra-Europe flights, which often have stricter and separate carry-on rules.
- **Charge everything and pack chargers**, a power bank, and a plug adapter — Italy uses Type C and F sockets at 230V, so non-European devices need an adapter (most modern electronics are dual-voltage, but double-check anything older, like hairdryers or straighteners).
- **Confirm your airport transfer or hotel pickup details** — driver contact info, meeting point, and flight number shared with the provider.
- **Do a document check**: passport, ID cards, printed/digital copies of confirmations, driver's license and IDP if you're renting a car.
- **Set up your phone plan or activate your eSIM** so it's ready the moment you land, rather than fumbling with settings at the airport.

## Day of Departure

- **Keep passport, boarding pass, and confirmations in one accessible place** — a single folder or phone wallet, not scattered across apps.
- **Arrive at the airport with adequate buffer** — most airlines recommend arriving at least 2–3 hours before an international departure, more during peak holiday travel.
- **Do final home checks**: thermostat, locks, lights on timers, mail/package holds, plants, pet care confirmed.
- **Screenshot or save your first day's plan** — hotel address, check-in time, and how you're getting from the airport, so you're not relying on data the moment you land.

## While You're in Italy: Don't Forget

A short, ongoing list to keep in mind throughout the trip, not just before it.

- **Validate paper regional train tickets before boarding.** Stamp them in the small yellow or green machine on the platform — an unvalidated paper ticket is treated as no ticket at all under Italian law, and conductors can fine you on the spot (minimum around €50, more if evasion is suspected). Digital tickets purchased through the Trenitalia or Italo app validate themselves automatically and don't need stamping.
- **Carry ID with you at all times.** Italian law requires it, and hotels, some attractions, and police checks may ask for it.
- **Keep tourist tax cash on hand.** Most Italian cities charge a per-person, per-night city tax (roughly €1–10 depending on the city and hotel category) that's often collected in cash at check-in or check-out rather than billed to your card.
- **Watch for ZTL zones if you're driving.** Zona a Traffico Limitato (limited traffic zone) signs mark historic centers in cities like Florence, Rome, and Milan where only authorized vehicles may enter. Cameras issue automatic fines — often €80–150 or more — that arrive by mail months later via the rental company. If you have a driver instead of a rental car, this simply isn't your problem.
- **Reconfirm each leg of transport a day ahead** — train platforms in Italy are sometimes announced only shortly before departure, so keep the app handy.
- **Keep a small cash buffer** for restrooms, small family-run restaurants, and markets that don't take cards.
- **Guard your valuables in crowded tourist areas** (train stations, popular squares, crowded buses) — pickpocketing is the most common issue tourists report, not violent crime.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How far in advance should I book Uffizi, Vatican, or Colosseum tickets?**
For peak season (April–October), book 3–4 weeks ahead at minimum; for July, August, or major holidays, 2–3 months ahead is safer, especially for the Vatican Museums and the Last Supper in Milan, which has extremely limited daily capacity.

**Do I need a visa to visit Italy?**
Many non-EU nationals, including US, UK, Canadian, and Australian citizens, can currently enter Italy visa-free for short tourist stays under Schengen rules. Requirements vary by nationality, so check your country's official travel advisory before booking.

**Is ETIAS required to enter Italy right now?**
No. ETIAS, the EU's planned pre-travel authorization for visa-exempt travelers, has been repeatedly delayed and is not yet in effect as of this writing. Watch official EU or national government sources for updates rather than third-party sites.

**Do I need to validate my train ticket in Italy?**
Yes, if you have a paper regional ticket — stamp it in the yellow or green machine on the platform before boarding. Digital tickets bought through official train apps are validated automatically and don't need stamping.

**What plug adapter do I need for Italy?**
Italy uses Type C and F plugs at 230V. A simple Type C adapter works for most sockets; check whether your device is dual-voltage before plugging in anything that draws significant power, like a hairdryer.

**Will my phone work in Italy without extra cost?**
Only if you're an EU/EEA resident under "Roam Like at Home" rules. Most other travelers should set up an eSIM or international plan before departure to avoid high roaming charges.

**What is the tourist tax and how do I pay it?**
Most Italian cities charge a small per-person, per-night occupancy tax, often €1–10 depending on the city and hotel category. It's frequently collected in cash at check-in or check-out rather than added to your card payment, so keep some cash available.

**Should I rent a car or hire a private driver in Italy?**
If your itinerary includes historic city centers, a private driver avoids ZTL restrictions, parking headaches, and unfamiliar signage entirely. For scenic countryside routes or day trips, a [chauffeur service](/chauffeur-service) offers the flexibility of a car without the risk of camera-issued fines.
