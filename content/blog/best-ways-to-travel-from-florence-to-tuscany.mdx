Florence is Tuscany's best-known city, but it's also, somewhat confusingly, not really "in" the Tuscany most travelers picture when they book the trip. The hill towns, vineyards, and countryside that fill postcards and Instagram feeds sit outside the city, which means almost every visitor ends up facing the same question at some point: what's the best way to actually get out there?

There isn't a single right answer — it depends on how much ground you want to cover, how much planning you want to do yourself, and how comfortable you are driving unfamiliar roads. Here's how the main options actually compare.

## Renting a Car

A rental car is the default choice for a lot of travelers, and it's easy to see why: it offers full control over the route and schedule, with no one else's timetable to work around.

The downsides are real, though, and worth weighing honestly. Driving in central Florence itself is difficult for visitors — much of the historic center is a restricted traffic zone (ZTL), and driving into it without authorization results in a fine, sometimes more than one if you cross the boundary repeatedly before realizing the mistake. Most travelers renting a car for Tuscany pick it up on the way out of the city rather than trying to navigate with it inside the center.

Once you're on rural roads, a rental car works well, but Tuscany's countryside routes are often narrow, hilly, and unfamiliar, and parking near hill town centers can be limited or require a walk uphill from the nearest lot. Fuel, tolls, and rental insurance also add to the cost in ways that aren't always obvious when comparing the sticker price of a rental against other options. If wine tasting is part of the plan, there's also the obvious problem of needing a sober driver for the return leg.

## Organized Bus Tours

Group bus tours are a low-effort option for travelers who'd rather not deal with logistics at all. Tickets are usually sold in advance, the route is planned for you, and there's no driving or navigation involved.

The tradeoff is flexibility. A bus tour runs on a fixed schedule, visits a fixed set of stops, and moves at the group's pace rather than yours — if you'd like an extra twenty minutes somewhere or want to skip a stop that doesn't interest you, that's generally not an option. Group sizes can also be large, especially during peak season, which changes the character of a countryside visit that a lot of travelers picture as quiet and unhurried.

For a first, general orientation to a couple of well-known towns, a bus tour can be a reasonable and inexpensive way to see the basics. For a trip built around a specific interest — a particular wine region, a slower pace, or towns that aren't on the standard tour circuit — it tends to feel restrictive.

## Trains to Specific Towns

Florence has excellent rail connections to some Tuscan destinations, and for a handful of towns, the train is a genuinely practical option. It's worth knowing which ones, though, because it's not a universal solution to getting around the region.

Here's a detail that catches a lot of travelers off guard: Siena, one of Tuscany's most visited cities, doesn't have a train station in its historic center. The station sits well outside the walled old town, down in a valley, and reaching the actual center from there requires a bus or a fairly steep uphill walk. It's manageable, but it's not the simple "get off the train and you're there" experience travelers sometimes expect.

Smaller hill towns and countryside areas — much of Chianti, for instance, or wine regions further south — often have no train access at all, or a station a considerable distance from the actual town. Trains work reasonably well for Florence-to-Pisa and a handful of other direct city connections, but they're a poor fit for the kind of multi-town, countryside-heavy day that draws a lot of travelers to Tuscany in the first place.

## Private Transfer or Chauffeur

A [private chauffeur](/chauffeur-service) sits between the extremes of a rental car and a fixed-schedule tour. You get a fixed, planned route without having to drive it yourself, but with far more flexibility than a bus tour or train connection allows.

This matters most for exactly the kind of day a rental car or train struggles with: multiple stops in one day, towns that aren't directly connected to each other by public transport, or a wine region visit where nobody wants to be the designated driver. A driver familiar with the region also solves the smaller, easy-to-overlook problems — where to park near a hill town's restricted core, which roads are actually practical during harvest season traffic, how long a specific drive realistically takes once you account for the terrain.

It's also the most adaptable option if plans change during the day. A rental car gives you that flexibility too, technically, but only if you're comfortable improvising a route on unfamiliar roads; a private transfer gives you the same adaptability without anyone in the car needing to navigate.

## Matching the Option to the Trip

- **A single focused day trip to one or two well-known towns:** a bus tour or, for towns with good rail access, a train can work fine and costs less.
- **A flexible day or multi-day trip covering several towns, wine country, or less-visited corners of the region:** a private transfer or chauffeur arrangement is generally the most practical option, since it avoids both the fixed schedule of a tour and the navigation burden of a rental car.
- **A trip where driving yourself is genuinely appealing** — a long stay with time to get comfortable with the roads, and no wine tastings on the agenda — a rental car remains a reasonable choice, provided you're prepared for the ZTL and parking realities around Florence and the hill towns themselves.

For travelers coming into Florence from elsewhere — Rome or Pisa, for instance — it's worth thinking about this question before the trip even starts, since [how you arrive in Florence](/blog/florence-airport-to-tuscany-private-transfer-guide) often shapes which of these options makes the most sense for the days that follow. ## The Bottom Line

There's no single best way to get from Florence into Tuscany — only the option that best fits the specific trip you're planning. A rental car suits travelers comfortable navigating unfamiliar roads and the city's ZTL; a bus tour suits a low-effort, fixed itinerary; trains work well for a short list of well-connected towns but not much beyond that; and a private transfer suits anyone who wants a flexible, multi-stop day without doing the driving or the planning themselves.
