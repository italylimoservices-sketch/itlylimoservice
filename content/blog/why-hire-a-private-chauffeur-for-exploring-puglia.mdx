Puglia looks like it should be easy to get around. It's a single, relatively narrow region running down the heel of Italy's boot, and on a map the distances between Bari, Alberobello, Ostuni and Lecce don't look dramatic. In practice, the region's charm is also what makes it awkward to cover without help: the best parts of Puglia are small towns, countryside estates, and coastal villages, and the connections between them weren't really built with visitors moving quickly between five different places in a week.

## Puglia's Towns Are Small, Scattered and Lightly Connected

Bari is the exception — a real city with a functioning transport network. Beyond it, Puglia's draws are mostly small towns: Alberobello and its UNESCO-listed trulli district, Ostuni climbing its hillside, Lecce with its baroque churches, and a string of smaller coastal towns and inland villages that rarely make it onto a first-time visitor's list but are often where the region feels most authentic. None of these are large enough to support frequent rail service, and buses connecting them tend to run on schedules built around local commuting patterns rather than tourist convenience.

This is the real case for a private chauffeur in Puglia — not that the region is hard to drive, exactly, but that stitching together public transport across several small towns in a single trip is often impractical. A missed connection or an infrequent bus schedule can cost the better part of a day, which matters a lot when you're only in the region for a week or less.

## What Self-Driving Actually Involves Here

None of this means self-driving is unreasonable. Plenty of confident travelers rent a car in Puglia and have a perfectly good trip. The region's main roads are generally in good condition, and a GPS handles most of the routing without much trouble. But a few things catch first-time visitors off guard:

- **Historic town centers often restrict vehicle access.** Lecce's old town and parts of smaller centers like Ostuni have limited or camera-enforced zones, and driving into one without realizing it can mean an unexpected fine.
- **Inland roads through the trulli countryside and olive groves can be narrow and poorly signed**, especially around Alberobello, where small rural roads sometimes carry more local tourist traffic than their width really suits.
- **Parking near the main sights in smaller towns is limited**, and visitors often end up circling for a spot rather than walking straight in.
- **Rural fuel stations can be sparse** and, like many Italian shops, sometimes close for a midday break — worth factoring in in the countryside rather than city.

None of these are dealbreakers for a comfortable, experienced driver. They're simply the kind of small friction that adds up across a multi-town itinerary, and that a local chauffeur navigates without a second thought.

## What a Chauffeur Actually Changes

The most direct benefit is that nobody in the group has to drive, which matters more on a multi-day Puglia itinerary covering several towns than it would for a single city visit. Narrow lanes through trulli countryside, tight turns in a hillside town like Ostuni, and the general unfamiliarity of driving abroad are all easier to handle as a passenger than as a first-time driver working it out in real time.

The second benefit is flexibility within a day. If Alberobello turns out to be worth an extra hour, or a quiet coastal stop catches your attention on the way to Ostuni, a private driver can adjust without the group needing to negotiate a rental car return deadline or worry about parking somewhere new. That kind of flexibility tends to matter more in Puglia than in a region with one or two big cities, simply because so much of the region's appeal is in smaller, less obvious stops that don't show up on a standard tourist map.

There's also a simpler point: arriving somewhere relaxed rather than having spent the last half hour squinting at signage in an unfamiliar language and hunting for parking. After landing at [Bari Airport](/airport-transfers), many travelers would rather hand the driving off entirely for the rest of the trip, particularly if the itinerary covers several towns in the days that follow.

## Where a Rental Car Still Makes Sense

It's worth being honest about the other side of this. If you're a confident driver, comfortable navigating with a GPS on unfamiliar roads, and you want the freedom to change plans without coordinating pickup times, a rental car is a reasonable choice for Puglia. It also tends to cost less over a longer trip, particularly if your itinerary is loosely planned and you expect to make spontaneous detours to smaller villages that wouldn't normally make a chauffeur's planned route.

A rental car tends to make the most sense when:

- You're traveling solo or as a couple without much luggage to manage
- Your itinerary is flexible rather than tied to specific timed stops
- You're comfortable with Italian road rules and narrow rural or historic-center driving
- You don't mind the trial and error of finding parking in small towns

A chauffeur tends to make more sense when the group is larger, when the trip includes airport or port connections that need precise timing, when the visit is tightly packed across several towns in a short window, or when the travelers would simply rather enjoy the olive groves and coastline than watch the road and hunt for parking.

## A Hybrid Approach Worth Considering

Some travelers split the difference: a chauffeur for the arrival leg and the first day or two while adjusting to the trip, then a rental car for a more loosely planned stretch later on, or the reverse — self-driving for a flexible first leg, then a chauffeur for a tightly scheduled wedding weekend or a day with several must-see stops. This isn't the obvious choice, but it works well for travelers who want some of each approach rather than committing to one for the entire trip.

## What First-Time Visitors Often Get Wrong

A common mistake among first-time Puglia visitors who choose to self-drive is underestimating how much time small towns actually take. A place like Alberobello looks like a quick stop on paper — a cluster of trulli, a short walk, done in an hour. In practice, parking outside the historic district, walking in, and finding your way back to the car can eat up more time than the sightseeing itself, especially in peak season when the town draws more visitors than its narrow streets were built for. Multiply that across three or four towns in a single day and a loosely planned self-drive itinerary can run long in a way that's hard to recover from.

This isn't an argument that self-driving doesn't work — plenty of visitors manage it just fine — but it's worth building real slack into a self-driven itinerary rather than assuming each stop will take as little time as it looks like it should. A chauffeur who already knows where a town's parking gets difficult, or which entrance avoids the longest walk, removes a layer of friction that's easy to underestimate from a map.

## The Honest Bottom Line

Puglia rewards whichever approach matches how you actually like to travel. If you want the freedom of your own schedule and don't mind the occasional wrong turn or parking search, a rental car will get you where you're going. If you'd rather spend the drive looking at olive groves and trulli rooftops instead of a map, and you want someone who already knows which small-town street turns into a no-entry zone, a private chauffeur is the more comfortable way to see the region. Either way, it's worth deciding before you land — Puglia's small towns have a way of taking longer to connect than the map suggests.
