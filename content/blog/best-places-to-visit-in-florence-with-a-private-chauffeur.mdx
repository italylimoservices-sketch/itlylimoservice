Florence's historic center is small enough to cross on foot in under half an hour, yet the city's best-known sights don't sit as close together as a map suggests. The Uffizi and the Ponte Vecchio share the same stretch of riverbank, but Piazzale Michelangelo sits on a hill across the Arno, and the Accademia Gallery is a genuine walk north of the cathedral. A day that tries to cover all of it on foot tends to turn into a lot of walking between short visits.

Here's a practical look at the best places to visit in Florence with a private chauffeur, grouped by how the sights actually sit on the map rather than by how famous each one is.

## Florence Is Compact, But Still Spread Out

Compared with Rome, Florence is genuinely walkable, and plenty of visitors cover the Duomo, the Uffizi and the Ponte Vecchio in a single afternoon without much trouble. Add Piazzale Michelangelo's hillside view or a stop in the Oltrarno's workshops, though, and the day starts to involve real distances, plus a climb up to Piazzale Michelangelo that isn't always welcome after hours of sightseeing already.

A chauffeur doesn't change what's in Florence. It changes how much of the day gets spent walking between places rather than standing in front of them, and it means the trip up to Piazzale Michelangelo happens by car instead of on foot at the end of a long day.

## The Duomo and the Historic Center

Florence Cathedral, known locally as Santa Maria del Fiore, sits at the heart of the historic center, and its dome is visible from much of the city. Piazza del Duomo is largely pedestrianized, so a chauffeur will typically drop you at the nearest accessible point and arrange a pickup nearby once you're ready to move on.

Because the Duomo sits roughly at the center of everything else on this list, it's a sensible place to start or finish a sightseeing day. Most other historic-center landmarks radiate outward from this square, which makes it a natural anchor point for planning the rest of the route. Traffic and pedestrian volume around the cathedral can vary through the day, so travel time to and from this area is worth treating as approximate rather than fixed.

## The Uffizi Gallery and Ponte Vecchio

A short walk south of the Duomo, the Uffizi Gallery and the Ponte Vecchio sit close enough together that they're usually treated as one stop. The Uffizi is one of Florence's best-known museums, and the Ponte Vecchio, the medieval bridge lined with small shops, is just a few minutes on foot from the gallery's exit.

This area gets busy, and vehicle access right along the riverbank is limited, so a chauffeur will generally drop you at the closest practical point and either wait nearby or return at an agreed time. Pairing these two stops means you can see both without arranging a separate transfer between them.

## The Accademia Gallery

The Accademia, home to Michelangelo's David, sits a little further north of the Duomo than the Uffizi does, in a quieter part of the historic center. It's worth knowing before you go that the Accademia requires advance, timed-entry booking, since demand to see the statue is consistently high. This is a stop worth building into a day's plan early, rather than treating as something you can drop into a loose schedule.

Because the entry time is fixed, having a chauffeur available afterward is particularly useful here. There's no need to work out onward transportation once your visit ends at a specific time, and the drive back toward the center or on to the next stop can happen right away.

## Piazzale Michelangelo

Piazzale Michelangelo sits across the river and uphill from the historic center, and it's known as one of the best places to take in an overview of Florence, with the Duomo's dome and the city's rooftops spread out below. Walking there is possible but involves a real climb, which is one of the more common reasons visitors choose to arrive by car, especially later in the day after other sightseeing.

A chauffeur can drop you close to the viewpoint and be waiting when you're ready to head back down, which matters more here than almost anywhere else on this list, since there's no easy flow of taxis at the top of a hillside piazza the way there is in the city center.

## The Oltrarno District

On the opposite bank of the Arno from the main historic center, the Oltrarno has a noticeably different atmosphere: narrower streets, artisan workshops, and a quieter, more local feel than the busy squares around the Duomo. It rewards slow wandering rather than a quick pass-through, so it's best treated as a stop where you're dropped at the edge of the district and explore on foot from there.

The Oltrarno pairs naturally with Piazzale Michelangelo, since both sit across the river from the main center, and a chauffeur can move between them in a few minutes rather than requiring a walk back across the Ponte Vecchio and up again.

## Vehicle Access in Florence's Historic Center

Florence's historic center has restricted traffic zones, commonly referred to as ZTL, that limit which vehicles can enter certain streets at certain times. This doesn't change what a visitor experiences day to day, since a chauffeur already knows the rules and where a drop-off near a given landmark is realistically possible, but it's part of why driving directly to the door isn't always an option around the Duomo and the oldest parts of the city.

## The Best Places to Visit in Florence, Grouped by Location

A common mistake when planning a Florence sightseeing day is listing stops in order of how well known they are instead of where they actually sit relative to one another. The Duomo, Uffizi and Ponte Vecchio sit close together in the historic core, the Accademia sits a short distance north, and Piazzale Michelangelo and the Oltrarno both sit across the river to the south. Visiting the Oltrarno in the middle of a day built around the Uffizi, then doubling back toward the Accademia afterward, adds driving time that a different order would avoid.

A chauffeur familiar with the city can suggest a sequence that moves in roughly one direction rather than crossing the river back and forth more than necessary, which matters more the more stops you're hoping to fit into a single day.

## Sequencing a Day Around Several of These

A reasonably efficient day covering several of the best places to visit in Florence might start with the Duomo and historic center in the morning, move to the Uffizi and Ponte Vecchio before midday, cross the river to the Oltrarno in the afternoon, and finish at Piazzale Michelangelo as the light starts to change in the early evening. The Accademia can be slotted in wherever its booked entry time falls, since that's the one stop on this list where the schedule isn't really yours to set.

## Conclusion

The best places to visit in Florence sit closer together than Rome's major landmarks do, but they still fall into different pockets of the city, separated by a river, a hill, and a historic center that doesn't always allow vehicles right up to the door. Grouping stops by location rather than by popularity, and having transportation ready rather than searched for after each visit, is what turns a good list of landmarks into a day that actually flows.
