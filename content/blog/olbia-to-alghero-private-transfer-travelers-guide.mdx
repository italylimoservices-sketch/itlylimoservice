Sardinia's northern coast has two distinct gateway towns that don't sit anywhere near each other: Olbia in the northeast, and Alghero in the northwest. Both have airports, both draw their own share of visitors, and a fair number of travelers find themselves needing to get from one to the other rather than treating them as separate trips entirely. This is a look at what that journey involves and how to think about planning it.

## Two Different Sides of the Same Coast

Olbia and Alghero both sit on Sardinia's north coast, but the character of the coastline between and around them shifts quite a bit. Olbia and the area around it — including the Costa Smeralda and towns like San Teodoro — has built a reputation around glamour, yachting, and a certain polish. Alghero, on the other hand, has a more Catalan-influenced old town, a different architectural feel left over from centuries of Aragonese and Spanish rule, and a coastline that leans toward dramatic cliffs and coves as you head further northwest toward places like Capo Caccia.

Travelers who want to see both sides of northern Sardinia rather than picking just one often book this route as a way to transition between two very different experiences within the same trip, rather than as a simple point-to-point errand.

## What Alghero Offers That Olbia Doesn't

If you're trying to decide whether the detour to Alghero is worth building into your Sardinia trip, it helps to know what you're actually trading. Olbia's draw is largely about access — it's the gateway to the Costa Smeralda, to San Teodoro, and to the yachting and resort scene that's made northeastern Sardinia famous. Alghero is a different kind of destination in its own right. Its old town has a genuinely distinct character, shaped by a long period of Catalan-Aragonese rule that left its mark on the local dialect, the architecture, and even some street names. Walking the historic center feels noticeably different from anywhere in the northeast of the island.

The coastline around Alghero also has its own identity, with limestone cliffs, sea caves, and a generally wilder feel the further northwest you go toward Capo Caccia, compared to the more manicured stretches near the Costa Smeralda. None of this means one side of the island is "better" — they're simply different enough that a lot of visitors find the transfer worthwhile rather than treating Sardinia's north coast as a single uniform strip.

## Who Tends to Book This Route

A few traveler patterns show up repeatedly for this transfer:

- **Multi-region Sardinia trips.** Visitors splitting their stay between the northeast (Olbia, Costa Smeralda, San Teodoro) and the northwest (Alghero and its surroundings) without wanting to backtrack through Cagliari.
- **Fly-in, fly-out itineraries.** Some travelers deliberately fly into one northern airport and out of the other, since Sardinia's two northern gateways make that possible without adding a long detour south.
- **Travelers prioritizing Alghero's old town and coastline** after spending initial days around Olbia's more resort-oriented atmosphere, looking for a change of pace partway through the trip.

## Practical Planning Notes

A few things worth keeping in mind regardless of why you're making this trip:

**Pack for a real drive, not a short hop.** Since timing isn't fixed and the route covers genuine distance, it's sensible to have snacks, water, and anything you'd want for a multi-hour car ride, particularly with children.

**Confirm your exact start and end points.** Whether you're departing from Olbia Airport, the city center, or somewhere further out near the coast, and whether you're headed to central Alghero, its airport, or a specific hotel, clarity on both ends avoids confusion on travel day.

**Think about timing on the Alghero end too.** If you have a flight out of Alghero Airport, or a check-in time at a property there, build in a reasonable buffer rather than assuming the drive will land exactly when hoped.
