Very few travelers arrive in northern Italy with Lake Como as the entire trip. It's usually one stop in a sequence that includes Milan on one side and, for some itineraries, a Swiss destination on the other, which means the real planning question often isn't "how do I get to Lake Como" but "where does Lake Como fit in everything else I'm doing." This guide steps back from any single route to look at that bigger picture — sequencing, luggage, and the logistics of moving through more than one place without losing momentum along the way.

## Lake Como's Position on the Map, and Why It Matters for Sequencing

[Lake Como](/destinations/lake-como) sits close enough to Milan that it works naturally as either a first stop after landing or a final stop before departure, and close enough to the Swiss border that it also works as a stepping stone toward destinations further north. That dual position is what makes it such a common piece of longer itineraries rather than a standalone destination — it connects easily in more than one direction, which gives travelers real flexibility in how they build the rest of the trip around it.

## Milan First or Milan Last

There's no universally right order, but the two options do feel different in practice. Starting in Milan and moving to Lake Como afterward front-loads the trip with a city pace before shifting into something quieter, which suits travelers who want to ease into a slower rhythm as the trip goes on. Doing it the other way — lake first, Milan last — means ending the trip with better airport access and a wider range of dining and last-minute shopping before an international flight, which some travelers prefer for the practical reasons alone. Neither order changes the drive between the two; it simply changes which city serves as your final base before heading home.

## Adding a Swiss Extension

For travelers with more time, Lake Como's proximity to the border opens up a genuine option to extend the trip into Switzerland rather than treating Italy and Switzerland as entirely separate visits. Lugano sits close enough to the lake to work as a relatively short addition, crossing at Chiasso in around 35 km and roughly 45 minutes to an hour under normal conditions — a manageable extension rather than a major undertaking. St. Moritz is a different proposition altogether: a genuine mountain destination reached via a longer international drive, better suited to travelers with a specific interest in the Engadin valley's alpine character than to those looking for a quick add-on. Deciding between the two — or skipping a Swiss leg entirely — is worth doing early, since it shapes how much time the rest of the itinerary has to work with.

## How Many Stops Is Too Many

It's tempting, once a trip includes Milan, Lake Como, and possibly a Swiss stop, to keep adding pieces, but every additional city adds a transfer, a check-in, and a repacking, and there's a real point of diminishing returns where more stops start to mean less time actually enjoying any of them. A trip that moves through three well-chosen bases, each given a proper stay, tends to leave travelers with a stronger sense of the region than one that adds a fourth or fifth stop purely because the geography allows it.

## Luggage Across a Multi-Stop Trip

Multi-city travel changes how luggage should be thought about from the start, rather than something to sort out at each individual transfer. A single main suitcase per traveler, packed with the assumption that it moves through every stop, tends to work better than assuming you'll repack extensively at each hotel. It's also worth being realistic that luggage accumulates — souvenirs, purchases, an extra bag picked up along the way — and that the vehicle suited to your group at the start of the trip may not be the same one that suits it by the final leg. Mentioning your full itinerary when you book each transfer, rather than treating each leg as an isolated booking, helps a chauffeur service anticipate that shift rather than react to it on the day.

## Choosing Vehicles for a Trip With Multiple Legs

Because luggage and group composition can shift over a multi-city trip, it's worth thinking about vehicle needs across the whole itinerary rather than just the first leg. An [executive sedan](/fleet/executive-sedan) or [luxury sedan](/fleet/luxury-sedan) suits a couple or solo traveler with standard luggage for most legs, up to 3 passengers and 2 suitcases. A [luxury SUV](/fleet/luxury-suv) offers more room, up to 5 passengers and 4 suitcases, which often makes sense once a trip picks up extra bags along the way. Larger groups traveling the full route together generally do better booking an [executive van](/fleet/executive-van) or [luxury van](/fleet/luxury-van) from the outset, each seating up to 7 passengers with 6 suitcases, rather than trying to resize partway through.

## Building in Time, Not Just Distance

A multi-city itinerary is easy to plan on a map and harder to plan on a calendar. It's worth resisting the urge to measure the trip purely in kilometers between stops and instead thinking about how much time each stop actually deserves — a rushed half-day in a city or lake town rarely leaves the impression its reputation suggests, while a slightly shorter list of stops, each given a real stay, usually produces a better trip overall.

## Booking the Whole Trip or One Leg at a Time

Some travelers prefer to lock in every transfer before departure, while others book leg by leg as the trip unfolds and plans firm up. Both approaches work, but if your itinerary includes an optional Swiss extension or a decision still to be made about ordering Milan and Lake Como, it's worth flagging that uncertainty when you first reach out through [contact](/contact) rather than waiting until the trip is already underway. A chauffeur service that knows the shape of your full trip, even with some pieces still undecided, can plan around that flexibility far more easily than around a series of unrelated last-minute requests.

## Conclusion

Lake Como rarely stands alone in a real itinerary, and planning it as one piece of a bigger trip — sequenced sensibly around Milan, weighed honestly against a possible Swiss extension, and supported by transfers arranged with the whole journey in mind — tends to produce a smoother trip than treating each leg as its own separate booking. Get the sequencing and the logistics right, and the transfers themselves become the easy part of a much bigger, more memorable trip through northern Italy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I visit Milan before or after Lake Como?**
Either order works. Milan first eases you from a city pace into a quieter one, while Lake Como first leaves Milan as your final base, which some travelers prefer for airport access and last-minute plans before flying home.

**Is it worth adding a Swiss destination to a Lake Como trip?**
It depends on your time. Lugano is a relatively short addition at around 35 km and 45 minutes to an hour from the lake, while St. Moritz is a longer mountain journey better suited to travelers specifically interested in that alpine destination rather than a quick add-on.

**How many cities should I try to fit into one northern Italy trip?**
There's no fixed number, but each added stop brings another transfer and repacking, and a trip with fewer, well-chosen stops given a proper stay generally leaves a stronger impression than one stretched across too many bases.

**Should I book all my transfers in advance or arrange them as I go?**
Both approaches work. If part of your itinerary is still undecided, such as whether to add a Swiss leg, it's worth mentioning that upfront through [contact](/contact) so transfers can be planned around your full trip rather than treated as isolated bookings.
