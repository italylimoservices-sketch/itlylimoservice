Most guides to visiting Venice for the first time list landmarks in isolation — St. Mark's Square, then the Rialto Bridge, then the Doge's Palace — as though you might visit them in any order. In practice, the best way to experience Venice's landmarks is as a single walking route, one that starts where private transportation leaves off and ends where it picks back up again. This is a look at **the best places to visit in Venice with private transportation**, organized the way you'll actually encounter them: as a sequence, not a checklist.

## Where the Vehicle Stops and the Walking Begins

Every version of this route starts the same way, regardless of where you're traveling from. A private transfer from [Marco Polo Airport](/airport-transfers/venice-marco-polo), a mainland hotel, or another Italian city brings you to Piazzale Roma, the square where Venice's road network ends. From there, the route that follows is entirely on foot or by water — there's no version of Venice sightseeing that involves a car once you're past this point, and no version of this guide pretends otherwise.

## Step One: Into the City Along the Grand Canal

From Piazzale Roma, the most natural way to begin is to follow the Grand Canal itself, either on foot along its banks and the streets that run parallel to it, or by vaporetto, which many first-time visitors find to be a memorable way to get a first look at the city — the palazzos, the bridges, and the general rhythm of water traffic all visible from the boat. This stretch functions as a kind of introduction to Venice before you reach any single named landmark.

## Step Two: The Rialto Bridge

The Rialto Bridge is a logical first proper stop on this route, since it sits along the Grand Canal itself and is usually reachable within a reasonable walk or a short vaporetto ride from Piazzale Roma. As the oldest of the bridges crossing the canal, it's a natural place to pause, look out over the water traffic, and get oriented before continuing deeper into the city. The streets and small squares around the bridge are also worth a slower look, since they tend to be busy with local market activity in a way that feels distinct from the more tourist-focused streets closer to St. Mark's Square.

## Step Three: Through the Streets Toward St. Mark's Square

From the Rialto, the route continues on foot through a network of narrow streets and small campos toward St. Mark's Square. This stretch of walking is where Venice's geography really asserts itself — there's no straight line between any two points in the historic center, and the route winds past shops, cafes, and canal crossings rather than following a direct path. Allow more time for this stretch than a map's straight-line distance would suggest, particularly if the streets are busy.

## Step Four: St. Mark's Square, the Basilica, and the Doge's Palace

St. Mark's Square is the natural centerpiece of the route, and it's worth treating it that way rather than rushing through. St. Mark's Basilica, with its domes and mosaics, sits at one end of the square, and the Doge's Palace, the former seat of Venice's government, sits just beside it. Because the two are so close together, most visitors treat them as a single extended stop, moving from the square into the basilica and then into the palace before returning outside.

## Step Five: The Bridge of Sighs

Tucked against the side of the Doge's Palace, the Bridge of Sighs is a small, enclosed bridge best appreciated either from inside the palace or from the nearby bridge that offers a clear view down the canal toward it. It's a brief stop, but a natural one to fold into the same visit as the square and the palace, since it sits only steps away from both.

## Where the Route Loops Back

Once the St. Mark's Square cluster is covered, most travelers either retrace part of the route back toward Piazzale Roma on foot, or take a vaporetto back along the Grand Canal, which tends to be the easier option after a few hours of walking. Either way, this is where the route closes the loop it opened at Piazzale Roma, bringing you back to the point where private transportation becomes relevant again.

## Where Private Transportation Picks Back Up

The entire walking route above happens without a vehicle of any kind, and that's simply how Venice sightseeing works. Private transportation reenters the picture at both ends of the route: getting you to Piazzale Roma to begin with, and picking you up again once you've made your way back, whether that's for a return to the airport, a transfer to a mainland hotel, or an onward drive to another Italian city. If your plans include a day trip beyond Venice, such as to [Verona](/destinations/verona), that's another point where a chauffeur genuinely drives you between places, in a way that isn't possible anywhere along the route itself.

## Adjusting the Route to Your Pace

This sequence works as a full day for most travelers, but it can be shortened by stopping after the Rialto Bridge and St. Mark's Square if time is limited, or extended by adding a slower wander through the quieter streets between stops rather than moving directly from one landmark to the next. Crowds, weather, and how long you choose to linger inside the basilica or the palace will all affect the overall pace, so it's worth treating any timing as a rough guide rather than a schedule to hit exactly.

## Why This Order Beats a Landmark List

Plenty of guides present these same five stops as an unordered list, leaving it to the traveler to figure out how they connect. The trouble with that approach is that Venice's streets don't reward improvisation the way a grid-based city might — doubling back to catch a landmark you skipped can mean crossing several bridges again rather than a quick detour. Following the route in the sequence above, from Piazzale Roma along the Grand Canal to the Rialto, then through the connecting streets to St. Mark's Square and the Bridge of Sighs, means you're always moving generally forward rather than zigzagging across the city to pick up something missed earlier.
