Alberobello is the kind of place that looks almost unreal in photos — entire streets lined with small whitewashed houses topped with grey conical roofs, each one stacked with hand-placed stones and no mortar holding them together. It's one of Puglia's most photographed towns and a near-automatic stop for anyone basing themselves in Bari. This guide covers what the town actually involves, how to think honestly about the drive, and a few practical details that make the visit smoother.

## What the Trulli Actually Are

The trulli (singular: trullo) are traditional dry-stone huts unique to this part of Puglia, and Alberobello's old quarters hold the largest and best-preserved concentration of them anywhere. UNESCO recognized the town's trulli districts as a World Heritage Site, and it's easy to see why once you're walking among them — the conical roofs, some marked with painted symbols, line narrow streets that climb a gentle hill.

The construction method is the interesting part. Builders stacked local limestone without mortar, which, as local guides often point out, made it easy to dismantle a trullo quickly — useful in centuries past when it meant avoiding certain taxes tied to permanent dwellings. Whether or not every version of that story is strictly accurate, it's part of the town's identity now, and it adds a layer of context to what might otherwise just look like a pretty architectural quirk.

## Rione Monti and Aia Piccola

Alberobello's trulli are concentrated in two main districts, and they feel quite different from each other.

**Rione Monti** is the larger and busier of the two, home to over a thousand trulli climbing the hillside opposite the modern town center. This is the district most souvenir shops, trullo-turned-boutiques, and small cafés occupy, and it's also where most day-trip groups spend their time. It's genuinely beautiful, but expect company, especially around midday.

**Aia Piccola** sits across the way and has a noticeably quieter, more residential feel. Many of its roughly two hundred trulli are still lived in rather than converted into shops, and wandering here gives a better sense of what the town might have felt like before tourism became the dominant industry.

Both districts are compact and easy to cover on foot, though the streets are cobbled and genuinely steep in parts, so footwear matters more here than in a flat coastal town.

## A Few Practical Notes

**Footwear matters.** The cobbled streets throughout both trulli districts are uneven, and the hillside layout of Rione Monti means a fair amount of gentle climbing. Comfortable shoes make a real difference.

**Midday brings crowds.** Alberobello is a popular stop for organized day trips, and the busiest window tends to fall around midday when multiple tour groups overlap in Rione Monti. An earlier start, where your schedule allows it, generally means quieter streets.

**Some trulli are private homes.** Especially in Aia Piccola, many of the structures are still residential. It's worth being mindful about photographing directly into open doorways or windows.

**Shops close for lunch.** As in much of southern Italy, some smaller shops in Alberobello observe a midday closure, so don't assume every storefront will be open straight through the afternoon.
